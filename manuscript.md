# Chapter One — The Feet

My feet are a pair of cunts, both of them.

That is the cleanest description I have found. If I am buying runners, I do not just buy runners. First I buy the things that make the runners possible. Soft insoles. Foot plasters. Enough of them to assume in advance that something is going to rub, press, blister or hurt. Proper shoes can become miserable within five minutes. I have worn runners to weddings. At weddings abroad I have worn sandals. There is no romance in this. Shoes are equipment. My feet have always required management. Athlete’s foot. Ingrown toenails. Corns. Calluses. Bruises. Nails that grew into strange shapes. Hammer toes. Pressure where there should not have been pressure. Pain that could become so local that I could point to the exact place, especially around the outside of the left foot, and know that this was where the day was going to be negotiated.

For a long time I thought this was simply what having my feet was like. That is one of the peculiarities of living inside a body over many years. You do not necessarily experience an abnormality as an abnormality. You experience it as Tuesday. You buy different shoes. You walk differently. You stop standing a certain way. You put a plaster over the place that hurts. You complain that your feet are sore. Then you get on with something else. Years later, medicine would acquire much more complicated words for parts of what had been happening to me. One of those words would be dystonia. Looking backwards, the lower-limb story begins to acquire a shape that I could not have seen while I was living it. But that is not how it began. It began with my feet.

And before my feet became a medical history, they belonged to a life. At twenty-five, I had most of what I wanted. I was in a relationship with a woman I loved. I had gone back to college to study psychology and maths. I was interested in positive thinking, motivation, personal development and spirituality. I was reading *A Course in Miracles*. I played darts. I worked in bars. I had friends. Some days were college and work. Some were college and darts. Some were work and darts. Sometimes I managed all three. I did not experience myself as a man waiting for an illness. Quite the opposite.

“People loved me. I was just so easy for people. I didn’t want anything from them. I had what I wanted.”

That is how I remember myself then. I had begun bar work on 10 May 1997, my brother’s birthday. At first there was nothing glamorous about it. Tables. Ashtrays. Glasses. Ice. Deliveries. Kegs. The physical underside of the bar before you become one of the people standing behind it. I loved the team. A busy pub is a machine made almost entirely of people. Ten or fifteen staff can spend a night managing hundreds of customers, and everybody is affecting everybody else. Someone is pulling pints. Someone is collecting glasses. Someone is looking for change. Someone has not turned up. Somebody is drunk. Somebody is angry. Somebody is lonely. Somebody wants to tell you a story you have heard twelve times. And all night you are standing. That mattered less to me then than it does now.

There was a period of my life when standing for hours, carrying stock, moving kegs, lifting cases of Coke, clearing bins full of bottles and then walking miles home was simply what a day contained. I was happy to walk anywhere. By about 1999 I wanted to get behind the bar. The manager told me he would think about it, but that I was a bit quick with my tongue. I probably gave him a smart answer. But I understood what he meant. Bar work became one of the places where I learned to manage my own character. My face is expressive. It always has been. If I think something is ridiculous there is a reasonable chance my face will announce the news before my mouth does. I could be sharp. If somebody irritated me, I could make them feel small. That was dangerous in a bar.

You might deal with three hundred people in a night. Six hundred. Whatever the number was, one difficult interaction did not remain one difficult interaction if you carried it forward. The next customer could see your face. The next ten could hear your tone. So you learned to get over yourself quickly. There was a practical morality in that. Do not make people small because you can. A person could come in dirty, homeless, sick, drunk, upset, confused, annoying, frightened. You dealt with the person in front of you. Most of the time I was good at that.

“Anybody who knew me liked me.”

There is arrogance in that sentence if you say it the wrong way. I do not mean it that way. I mean that I was socially easy. I liked people. I was funny. I liked language. I liked reaction. A bar gave all of that somewhere to go.

“There’s five people looking at you laughing, and there’s two in the corner who’d begrudge you.”

That is an audience. I never thought of it as one. I was simply working. At the King’s Head I eventually became, in my own words, “the top man.”

“My name was the name.”

Again, I do not mean I walked around Galway thinking I was royalty. It was a place where I had standing. I knew the work. People knew me. I knew people. I could handle myself. There was physical confidence in that life too. The body did not yet need an explanation. Then, somewhere around the middle of my twenties, the feet began to cost more. My memory places the major pain around 2004 or 2005. The dates are not perfectly clean. Bodies do not issue commencement notices. There was pain around the outside of the left foot. There were corns and calluses. The toes could claw and cramp. Looking back now, I can see behaviour in the feet that I understand differently. At the time, I mostly had sore feet. That is an important distinction.

I did not live those years thinking: I have a neurological movement disorder in my feet. I lived them thinking: my fucking feet hurt. This was still a period when I could work long hours, walk miles and play darts. The body had not collapsed. It had become expensive. Darts exposed that in a peculiar way. I loved the act of throwing. There is something satisfying about it that does not require philosophy. You look at something. You throw something at it. You hit it. And then you do it again. I could play well. Fifteen-dart legs were within me. I liked the numbers, the aiming, the competition, the pub, the team. But darts also required a repetitive sequence that my feet increasingly disliked. Throw. Walk forward. Retrieve. Turn. Walk back. Set yourself. Stop. Start. Throw again. The movement between throws became part of the contest.

As an evening went on, backing away from the board, stopping, starting, clawing and turning would wear me down. It was not a dramatic seizure of the body. That would almost have been easier to identify. It was attrition.

“It’s like a man walking his final steps.”

By the end of some nights I could be leaning forward like a caveman. Exhausted. And still wanting another throw. That matters to me because illness stories are often rewritten afterwards as though the sick person must have spent years watching himself become sick. I did not. I spent years playing darts. I spent years working. I spent years drinking pints and talking shite and meeting people and going home and getting up and doing it again. Between roughly 2004 and 2012, I said remarkably little about how much difficulty I was having. I said my feet were sore. That was true. It was simply not complete. Only a tiny number of people directly commented on the way I was struggling, despite the number of people I encountered through work and pubs and darts. Perhaps that says something about how gradually a body can alter.

Perhaps it says something about how little we study one another’s gait. Perhaps I was better at concealing it than I knew. Perhaps everyone had their own life. In Taaffes, concealment did not make the work lighter.

“It’s crazy how much I neglected myself in Taaffes.”

The job was physically harder on me. Long shifts. Stock. Floors. Kegs. Cases. Bins. Hours standing before the walk home had even begun. I might walk roughly three miles to work. At the end of a late shift, often around one in the morning, there might be four pints, cigarettes, and then home. By then some of the social world of my twenties had begun to rearrange itself. Friends partnered off. People settled. Shifts changed. Lives diverged. The bar could contain hundreds of people and still leave you walking home alone. I drank regularly. That has to belong to the story without either melodrama or sanitising. There were periods when drinking was social, periods when it was habitual, periods when it belonged to loneliness, and periods when it had a bodily purpose I did not fully understand.

When tension became severe, alcohol could feel like one of the things that made a body easier to inhabit. The important thing is not to force the later explanation backwards. At the time I was not conducting a pharmacological experiment on myself. I was having pints. Sometimes I was stoned. Sometimes I was drunk. Sometimes I was both. And sometimes the body felt easier. If there is a temptation in telling this story now, it is to make everything point neatly towards July 2017. It did not. The road to diagnosis is obvious only after someone has put up the sign. For years, the feet existed in their own category because that is where I had put them. Bad feet. Painful feet. Ugly feet. Difficult shoes. Calluses. Corns. Toes that behaved badly. A left foot that could become vicious. A body that could still work. That last part is important.

I worked physically for years while this was happening. I carried heavy things. I stood through shifts. I walked long distances. I played darts. The existence of endurance did not mean the absence of disease. It meant I endured it. There is a Christian story called *Footprints in the Sand*. A person looks back across a life and sees two sets of footprints for most of the journey but only one set during the worst periods. He asks God why He abandoned him when life became hardest. God tells him those were the times He carried him. I have my own version.

“Well, in my hard times, I walked myself. That single set of footprints on the beach in my hard times, they’re mine.”

I do not say that because I think I am uniquely heroic. Mostly I find it funny. But there is something in it. Walking became one of the measurements of my life long before anybody treated it as a neurological sign. How far? In what shoes? How sore afterwards? How much was left? Could I get home? Could I stand again tomorrow? Those were not clinical questions. They were Tuesday. Years later I would read records in which gait became a neurological object. Festinant. Stiff. Difficulty initiating. Abnormal. Strikingly abnormal. Almost dystonically unusual. By then other people had acquired language for something I had already been living. But language changes the thing it describes. Once you call a gait Parkinsonian, everybody looks at the gait. Once you call a foot structural, everybody looks at the foot. Once you call pain neuropathic, everybody looks for neuropathy.

Before any of those divisions existed, there was simply me walking on my own feet. That is where I want to begin. Not in a hospital. Not with a scan. Not with a neurologist. Not even with Parkinson’s disease. With a man who could work a crowded Galway bar, carry a keg, walk home, throw darts, make people laugh, read about God, love a woman, drink four pints, neglect himself, and still believe the biggest problem with his feet was that they were a pair of cunts. For years, that explanation was enough. Then the rest of my body began to join them.

# Chapter Two — My Face Was Fine

The strange thing about becoming visibly unwell is that the face can keep getting away with it. Mine did.

My face was fine.

That was the problem and, for a while, it was also the advantage. A person can be funny while losing balance. He can be quick with language while his fingers are slowing down. He can walk into a television studio, make people laugh, solve numbers in his head, find words faster than the people beside him, and still be thinking carefully about where to put his feet because somebody has left cables across the floor. The public version of a person and the physical version can separate long before anybody gives the separation a name. By the time I got onto Countdown, I had already tried twice before.

I had loved the programme for years. It suited something in me. Words laid out in front of you. Numbers that either worked or did not. A clock. A task. No need to pretend that one answer was as good as another when it plainly was not. My mind works differently from normal when it comes to anagrams, puzzles, riddles and games. I do not mean that as a diagnosis. It is simply something I have always noticed about myself. Give me nine letters and sometimes the shape appears before the explanation does. Give me a puzzle and I can often see the route through it before I can tell you how I saw it. Numbers under a thousand have always been friendly territory too. I do not say that modestly because modesty is useless where arithmetic is concerned. I'm just good at it.

That was how I thought about the programme. Not: wouldn't it be extraordinary if I could survive on television. More: this is something I can do. Before filming I got stage fright. That was new enough to be annoying. I remember going back to my room and realising that if I spent the whole run-up frightened of the experience, I could spoil the experience before it happened and spend the rest of my life regretting that I had spoiled it. So I reduced it. I already looked at letters every day. I already talked to them in my head. I already did the numbers. The studio was only a room in which I had to do the thing I knew how to do. That worked. Mostly. The part I had not practised was walking onto the set. There were cables on the floor. I noticed them immediately.

Nobody should have to notice cables as a threat to dignity. A television floor is not an assault course. People walk across it without narrating the process to themselves. Their balance does what balance is meant to do, which is stay out of the conversation. Mine had started entering the conversation. I had lived with foot pain for years by then. There was tension in my legs. There were things happening in the feet I could not name. Sometimes a step felt straightforward until it did not. Sometimes the outside of the left foot could punish me sharply enough to alter the next movement. I was not walking onto the set thinking any of this was part of one grand illness. I was thinking about getting to the chair without making a show of the walk before the show had even begun. My physicality was failing at that stage.

My face was fine.

My body was beginning to not trust itself. That distinction mattered because once I was seated, I could become myself again. The clock started. The letters came up. The numbers came up. The body moved to the background. That is one of the mercies of competence. When you know how to do something, the knowing can temporarily give you a place to stand even when standing is becoming complicated. I won six games. People later asked whether the wins increased my confidence. Not particularly. I already knew I was good at the game. The more interesting thing was what happened outside the studio. Countdown names places. Galway is not shy about hearing its own name. The programme went out and people began to notice.

Pubs showed the episodes. People sang the Countdown theme tune at me on buses. Strangers stopped me. Somebody approached me in Boots. The thing became locally ridiculous in the nicest possible way. Some people called it County Final Day. Others compared it to Italia '90. This was Galway. A man could win a few rounds on an afternoon words-and-numbers programme and, for a week, be treated as if he had come back from Rome with Packie Bonner. I enjoyed the absurdity. It also created another test of something bar work had taught me: meet people where they are. If somebody was delighted to see me because I had found a seven-letter word on television, then that delight was real even if the achievement itself had not rearranged my identity. You do not need to mock somebody's enthusiasm merely because you understand the scale of the event better than they do.

The best memory is not from the studio. It is from Kennedy's. My father was there. The horse racing finished and the pub replayed that day's episode. I was playing an English contestant called Karl. The letters came up: G-E-A-R-P-I-P-D. I declared seven. The pub cheered. Karl declared eight. The pub booed. This had become theatre now. Galway had made a word round tribal. I knew there was no proper eight-letter word there. My seven was gripped. Karl offered drippage. Drippage. The room reacted exactly as a room should react when an Englishman tries to introduce drippage into the language. There was cheering, booing, laughing, noise. And there was my father. What I remember most is the glee on his face. That is how memory behaves when it is kind. It does not keep the score. It keeps the face.

At the same time, I was working at Lake Region Medical. This was a different test entirely. Countdown rewarded exactly the parts of me that were still working well. Lake Region Medical exposed the parts that were not. The job involved fine motor work. That phrase sounds harmless until you put the task back into a pair of hands. You could be dealing with a twisted copper pair, guide wire, tiny materials, heat shrink, very precise binding, the sort of work where understanding the procedure is only the beginning. Your hands have to translate intention into sequence. You have to hold one thing while moving another thing while not damaging the thing you are trying to build. I liked that kind of problem. I could understand it. I just couldn't do it. That was different from not knowing how.

There is a particular frustration in understanding a task perfectly while the body refuses to perform the understanding. The mind sends the instruction. The fingers return an error. Even simpler things were becoming strange. Loose sheets of paper. Gather them. Tap the edges. Align them. Nothing. Try again. The kind of movement that should not qualify as a task could become one. Someone later asked me about embarrassment. Embarrassment it wasn't. Apathy it was. That answer matters. Embarrassment would have meant I was still negotiating with the opinion of the room. Apathy was something duller. You get tired of being surprised by the same body. You stop dramatizing every failure because there are too many of them. The sheet of paper does not care how intelligent you are. The wire does not care that you know the sequence.

The lid does not care that you opened ten thousand jars before this one. Things that had once been beneath attention began collecting attention like tax. At work, people could see some of it. One woman in particular urged me to get checked. She was somebody I liked and trusted. She said I reminded her of her son and pointed toward her head. It was an alarming gesture delivered cautiously enough that I did not quite know what to do with it. The next morning I asked her what she had meant. She told me her son had died of a brain injury. That landed differently. People say many things in workplaces. They diagnose each other over tea. They explain backs, knees, nerves, diets, stress, marriages and blood pressure with the confidence of men leaning against vending machines. This was not that.

This was somebody who had seen something serious before and was telling me, carefully, that I should not ignore what she could see. So I listened. Not immediately enough to turn into a saint of self-preservation. But I listened. By then I was living back at home with my parents and two brothers. The broader slowness was no longer easy to miss. Jars could be difficult. Foil packets could be difficult. Movement could be difficult. The feet were still sore. But there was now more than the feet. The body had begun expanding the complaint without asking my permission. This is the point in stories where the writer is tempted to make the man more frightened than he was. I was not walking around thinking: something catastrophic is happening.

Different possibilities had been mentioned around me. MS. Motor neuron disease. Other things. None of them had settled into me as identity. What I knew was simpler. I was getting slower. Things were harder. My feet hurt. My walk could go wrong. My hands were less dependable. And still, if you put nine letters in front of me, I might beat you. That was the imbalance. There is something almost insulting about how selectively a body can fail. It does not consult your idea of fairness. It does not say: because balance is worsening, I will also remove your humour so that everybody understands the seriousness. It does not say: because your fingers are failing, I will lower your confidence in arithmetic to match. The person remains uneven. That unevenness can fool everybody, including the person himself.

If the mind is fast, the sentence is sharp and the face is alive, the failing body can look like a secondary problem. Something to be worked around. Something to be endured. Something that will probably be fixed once somebody competent finally gets hold of it. That was still my assumption. In 2017, when I entered hospital, a nurse gave me stacks of five-cent coins. The idea was that I should practise stacking them to improve dexterity. I understood the logic. I rejected the premise. No, I told her. I'm okay. I don't need to learn how to do things when I'm not okay. I will be okay. That sentence contains more of the man I was than I understood at the time. It was confidence. It was denial. It was optimism. It was impatience with adaptation. It was faith in correction.

It was the assumption that this body, however troublesome, was still fundamentally on its way back to me. I did not yet understand that medicine was about to give me a different kind of sentence. A sentence with a name in it. By then, the public version of me had already been on television. Galway had cheered gripped and booed drippage. My father had laughed. My hands had failed at work. My balance had entered the conversation. A colleague had pointed at her head. The feet had been hurting for years. And my face was still fine.

# Chapter Three — The GP Watches It Change

The first doctor to see the shape of what was happening to me did not begin with Parkinson’s disease. He began with my feet. On 10 April 2017 I went to Galway East Medical Practice complaining of bilateral foot pain and an abnormal gait. That is how the record begins. Not with tremor. Not with bradykinesia. Not with a neurological diagnosis. Feet. Gait. Pain. I had recently transferred to the practice. I was still explaining the walk to myself in the language I had available. I said I thought it had started after a sciatica problem in the left leg years earlier and that perhaps the gait had become “habitual,” because I no longer had pain in my back or leg. That was my explanation then. The GP did not ridicule it and he did not adopt it as an answer. He looked.

He recorded marked mallet toes, onychogryphosis, callouses and pressure areas. I worked on my feet and was finding it tough. He planned a podiatry referral, ordered blood tests and wanted my old notes. It would have been easy to leave the story there. A man with bad feet goes to a GP. The GP sees bad feet and sends him to Podiatry. Except the walk was already saying something else. Over the next eleven weeks, Aaron Brennan watched the case change. That is the part of the story the later referral compresses into a few paragraphs. The referral is excellent, but before it became a letter it was a sequence of encounters in which one doctor had to decide whether what he was seeing was still adequately explained by the feet in front of him. On 1 June I came in with a cough.

I worked in a clean room and could not work while coughing, so the consultation was about an acute respiratory infection and a work certificate. There is nothing dramatic about that. It is exactly the kind of ordinary visit from which medicine is largely made. A week later, on 8 June, the cough was settling. The conversation moved elsewhere. Movement. The record says there was a problem with the fluidity of initiation, including getting started from sitting. I gave an example from the bus. If I was sitting down as the bus approached my stop, I would time getting up so that the slowing of the bus helped propel me forward. I had found a way to borrow momentum from public transport.

That sentence belongs in a medical record because I said it to a doctor. But before it belonged to medicine it was simply one of the small tricks by which I had learned to move through a day. The GP also recorded that I was conscious of my movements and thought a lot before moving. That is what happens when an automatic act stops being automatic. Walking begins in the head before it begins in the legs. And then Brennan wrote the phrase that, for me, defines the quality of his observation. He was considering a neurological opinion about my **“gait, feet and ‘muscle tension’ issues.”** Gait. Feet. Muscle tension. Together.

He did not yet know what the diagnosis was. He wanted my old notes because there had been some previous work-up. That was not hesitation born of indifference. It was an attempt to understand a trajectory before declaring what it meant. Four days later, on 12 June, the threshold changed. I was due to be reviewed through occupational health about my feet. Dr Cashman saw me there and telephoned Brennan about my gait. Brennan recorded the call. Dr Cashman “echoes my concerns.” That mattered. A second doctor had now independently looked at the same man and become concerned about the way he moved. Brennan no longer waited for the old notes. He decided to refer me to Neurology. The wording is simple: refer now, even before the old notes, because Cashman echoed the concern. There is no flourish in it. But there is judgment. The problem had crossed a line.

For years my feet had hurt. For years I had adapted. Now a newer component was becoming visible enough that two doctors, looking from different places, were concerned by the gait itself. This is the distinction that matters. The long lower-limb story was not suddenly replaced by Parkinsonism. A Parkinsonian component was arriving late into an older story. Brennan was watching that happen. He lectures aspiring GPs. I mention that not to decorate him with authority but because the method on the page deserves attention. He did not need to know the final answer in order to handle uncertainty well. He kept the dimensions alive. He watched change. He listened to explanation. He looked for corroboration. He escalated when the trajectory changed. And he did not throw away the feet when the neurological signs became more obvious.

On 20 June I had been off work for three weeks, initially because of a cough and then a flu-like illness. That distinction matters. It would be easy, from the future, to convert every absence from work into neurological disability. The record does not support that. The respiratory illness was real. But while I was off, the other problem continued developing. On 23 June I was planning to return to work the following Monday. By then I had been thinking about the gait myself. The clean room had become difficult because gowning and ungowning demanded exactly the kind of ordinary sequence that my body was making less ordinary. I was considering asking my employer for a different role. I had also started wondering whether alcohol might be playing some part.

The record is precise. I was not described as drinking very heavily. I said I had been drinking most nights to help “relax muscles.” That is an important sentence because it preserves two things at once. Alcohol was present. So was the symptom it was being used against. The drinking did not create the report of muscle tension after the fact. The muscle tension was part of why I said I was drinking. Brennan examined me. Marked increased tone in both legs. Brisk reflexes in the upper limbs. The story had moved again. This was no longer merely a man who said his feet were bad. There were objective signs in the body above the feet. The neurological referral had already been made. Six days later, on 29 June, I tried to go back to work. I could not do the job.

The record says I “essentially could not perform” because my dexterity felt worse. That is the point at which the trajectory stops being an accumulation of worrying signs and becomes functional collapse. The hands had now joined the feet. The work mattered because Lake Region Medical was not asking me to describe myself. It was asking me to perform. Gown up. Gown down. Sort papers. Handle small components. Fit scalpel blades to handles. Repeat simple manual tasks. The body either could or could not translate intention into action. By 29 June, it could not do so reliably enough for the job. Brennan telephoned the hospital. He spoke with Dr Gallagher. I was accepted for assessment in the Acute Medical Unit on Monday morning, 3 July, at 8:30. And then Brennan wrote the referral.

Years later, reading it, what strikes me is not simply that it contains a great deal of information. It is that it contains the **shape of the change**. He notes that when I transferred to the practice in April he had already noticed an unusual festinant and stiff gait. I had been attending with bilateral foot problems and, at that time, blamed the gait on callouses and corns. My feet, he wrote, were “not in a good state.” Then comes the temporal turn.

“Over recent weeks.”

That phrase matters. Over recent weeks it had become increasingly evident that I was having difficulty initiating movement. There was marked stiffness and increased tone in both upper and lower limbs. Brisk reflexes. Possible cogwheeling. No overt resting tremor. I described being unable to relax my muscles, especially in bed, and clawing my toes. Then work. I had been doing all right, but gowning and ungowning around the clean room had become increasingly difficult. Dexterity had worsened. Sorting papers into files had become difficult. Repetitive manual tasks that I had previously been capable of were failing. Then the question.

“?parkinsonian or other underlying diagnosis.”

Then the conclusion. I “certainly seems to be deteriorating.” The grammar is imperfect. The observation is not. This is what I mean when I say the referral was high-dimensional. It did not contain one symptom waiting for one diagnosis. It contained a trajectory. Old feet. Old gait difficulty. My own earlier explanations. A recent change in movement initiation. Increasing tone. Stiffness. Brisk reflexes. Toe clawing. Loss of dexterity. Work failure. Deterioration over time. And an open diagnostic question. Brennan did not refer Parkinson’s disease. He referred a man whose old lower-limb problem had acquired a new neurological dimension. That is why the temporal structure matters so much. If you read only the last page, you see a good referral. If you read the weeks that produced it, you see what the referral actually carries. On 10 April the feet and gait are foregrounded. On 8 June movement initiation joins them.

On 12 June another doctor independently confirms concern about the gait. On 23 June abnormal tone and reflexes are documented while I am contemplating changing work because of what the body is doing. On 29 June dexterity fails badly enough that I cannot perform my job, and hospital assessment is arranged. The story accelerates. For years, the body had changed slowly enough for me to adapt to it. Now the medical record changes over weeks. And through that acceleration, one thing remains unusually intact. The relationship between the parts. The GP does not decide that because Parkinsonism is becoming plausible, the older feet must belong somewhere else. He does the opposite. He sends the old and the new forward together. That is what a trajectory is. It is not a list of symptoms.

It is the order in which they appeared, the way they changed, the way one domain of the body began joining another, and the fact that an explanation adequate to April may no longer be adequate in June. The referral carried all of that into the hospital. If it landed as written, the hospital was not receiving two patients. It was receiving one man with one unresolved, changing presentation. Feet. Gait. Tone. Toes. Hands. Work. Time. The disease had not yet been named. But the problem had been preserved. Four days later, at about half past five in the evening, a neurologist would look at me and give the dominant part of it a name.

“You’ve got Parkinsonism.”

The diagnosis would become more sophisticated from there. The story would become narrower.

# Chapter Four — Parkinsonism

At about half past five on Monday evening, 3 July 2017, a neurologist looked at me and said:

“You’ve got Parkinsonism.”

That is how I remember the first encounter. The word landed before the explanation. I knew Parkinson’s disease as a thing that existed in other people’s lives. Parkinsonism was close enough to sound serious and unfamiliar enough that I did not yet know what distinction was being made. I did not hear it as an answer to everything that had been happening to me. I heard it as a name for what he could see. And, in one important sense, that was progress.

For years I had lived inside a body whose difficulties had accumulated without a single organising explanation. The feet had been painful for years. The gait had altered. Starting movement had become harder. My hands were becoming unreliable. Work had become difficult. By the end of June my GP had watched the newer movement disorder declare itself on top of that older lower-limb history and had sent the whole trajectory forward. Now I was in hospital and somebody was telling me that at least part of it had a neurological name. I trusted that. Why wouldn’t I? You do not go into hospital hoping the specialists will be baffled by you. You go because you want them to recognise something. They recognised something quickly. The neurological examination did not treat my walk as ordinary.

“The gait is strikingly abnormal.”

That is the phrase in the record. There was a footdrop-like appearance, but the note was emphatic:

“NOT FOOT DROP.”

The lower-limb posture was considered potentially dystonic. The left leg kicked out. There was bradykinesia. There were flat feet and hammertoes. My movement was visibly, clinically abnormal. This matters because the hospital did not fail to see that I was neurologically unwell. Quite the opposite. It saw it. It took it seriously. The work-up was substantial. There was imaging of the brain. Imaging of the spine. A CT brain. Blood investigations. A Parkinson-related panel. Ceruloplasmin. B12. Folate. Ferritin. Electrophoresis. An abdominal ultrasound looking for splenic enlargement associated with one of the disorders being considered. Physiotherapy saw me. Occupational therapy saw me. A DaT scan became part of the investigation. The machine of specialist medicine had started moving. For somebody who had spent years translating the body into logistics — shoes, distance, work, rest, another pint, another attempt — there was something reassuring about seeing the problem become worthy of machinery.

The scans did not produce an easy structural answer. The brain MRI was essentially normal. The spinal imaging did not show the sort of lesion that would neatly explain what was happening. The CT did not reveal an acute intracranial cause. So the question moved. Not away from neurology. Deeper into it. I was thirty-nine. That was young for the syndrome they were looking at. The movement pattern was severe enough to be obvious. The presentation was sufficiently unusual that the doctors began thinking beyond ordinary idiopathic Parkinson’s disease. Very young onset mattered. Symmetry mattered. The absence of a dominant family history mattered. My cognition mattered. The hospital was not merely attaching a familiar label to a strange walk. It was beginning to ask what kind of Parkinsonism this might be. That distinction is important to everything that follows.

It would be easy, knowing where this book goes, to write these doctors as though they were incurious. They were not. The problem is more interesting than that. The diagnostic thinking around Parkinsonism became increasingly sophisticated. At the same time, the shape of the case began to simplify. The GP had referred a trajectory. By the time the hospital story was being summarised, it could be expressed more cleanly: Difficulty initiating movement. Bradykinesia. Reduced dexterity. Impaired balance. Unsteady gait. All of those things were true. That is what makes compression difficult to see while it is happening. Nothing has to be invented. Nothing has to be denied. A history can lose dimensions while every sentence in the shorter version remains accurate. The feet were still there. The hospital record says clonazepam was given at night to relieve tension in my legs.

It says a podiatry referral was sent to review “issues with feet reported by the patient.” So the feet had not disappeared. But they were beginning to move differently through the system. The Parkinsonian features belonged at the centre of Neurology. The feet were increasingly becoming something reported by me, something for Podiatry, something adjacent. I did not see that distinction then. I had no reason to. From inside the bed, it looked like the hospital was working through the problem. A doctor had looked at me and recognised Parkinsonism almost immediately. People were examining me. Tests were being ordered. Scans were being done. Specialties were appearing. The very fact that the investigation was becoming complicated made me more, not less, confident that somebody was holding the whole thing. That is how trust works in a place like a hospital.

You do not personally integrate the MRI, the examination, the referral, the bloods, the gait, the feet, the history and the specialist differential. You assume that is what the hospital is doing. I certainly did. Treatment was also beginning. Clonazepam was used for the tension in my legs; rasagiline and procyclidine would become part of the plan after discharge. I was not in hospital to audit the architecture of my own case. I wanted to get better, and everything about the intensity of the investigation suggested that I had finally arrived somewhere capable of understanding what was happening. And the Parkinsonism was real. But I did not know that yet. What I knew was that a neurologist had seen me and named something serious. The hospital had treated the Parkinsonian component as real. The gait was not being dismissed. The slowness was not being dismissed.

The dexterity loss was not being dismissed. The movement disorder was being investigated with increasing sophistication. For the first time in years, the body seemed to be yielding information to people who knew what to do with it. I did not feel failed. I felt found. That distinction is essential. Because what happened next did not begin with distrust. It began with the opposite. I had arrived with a body I did not understand. Within hours, the hospital understood something important about it that I did not. That is why I trusted what came next. They had seen Parkinsonism. I assumed that meant they were seeing me.

# Chapter Five — Full NCT, Bald Tires

For eight days, the hospital looked almost everywhere. That is what the record shows. It looked at my brain. It looked at my cervical spine. It looked at my blood. It looked at copper. It looked at autoimmune disease. It looked at antibodies with names I had never heard before and would not have known how to pronounce. It looked at whether I might have a rare disorder associated with an enlarged spleen. It looked at the possibility of a toxic cause. It looked at my childhood head injury. It looked at my smoking. It looked at my drinking. It looked at recreational drugs I had taken years before. It looked at my handwriting. It looked at my eye movements. It looked at my blink rate. It looked at my sense of smell. It looked at my swallowing. It looked at my bowels. It looked at my urine.

It looked at my cognition. It looked at my mood. It looked at my religion. It looked at the possibility that my Parkinsonism was genetic. It looked at the possibility that it was not the ordinary kind of Parkinson's disease at all. This was not a hospital doing nothing. That matters. I had arrived on Monday, 3 July 2017 because my GP had watched an old lower-limb problem become something larger. By the end of June he had put the feet, abnormal gait, difficulty initiating movement, increased tone, brisk reflexes, toe clawing, failing dexterity and work deterioration into one urgent referral. He did not refer Parkinson's. He referred a trajectory.

The hospital received that trajectory and summarised it more cleanly: thirty-nine years old, difficulty initiating movement, increased tone and stiffness in all four limbs, cogwheeling at the wrist, neurological assessment required. Every surviving fact was true. The relationship between the facts was smaller. The feet and toe clawing had lost their force before the specialist investigation had properly begun. That is what compression looks like when nothing in the shorter version has to be false. I did not know any of that on the ward. I was not comparing documents. I was wearing hospital clothes and walking badly. I was waiting for people who knew more than I did to tell me what was happening. And they were working. The history they took was long. Long enough to reach back thirteen years. I told them about tension in the body going back to around 2004.

I told them that I did not synchronise my arms and legs properly when I walked. I told them my writing had deteriorated until it could become illegible. I told them my balance was very bad and that I believed a fall was coming. I told them my dexterity was diminished. I told them about the strange relationship between tension and relaxation in bed. And I told them about the feet. Corns. Callouses. Fallen arches. Treatment going back years. Pain. The fact that the feet remained a problem. The record even preserves the way I had tried to make sense of them myself. When I left bar work, some of the foot pain had improved. The leg tension had not. I had therefore begun to think the foot pain and the tension might not be the same thing. That was my interpretation then. It was not a neurological conclusion.

It was a man trying to understand his own body from inside it. The hospital wrote it down. Then came the examination. The tone was markedly and symmetrically increased. A resting tremor eventually appeared in the right leg. Reflexes were brisk. My facial expression was relatively good. My blink rate was markedly reduced. My eye movements were good. My coordination was slow. There was lead-pipe rigidity. Axial rigidity. Minimal cogwheeling. Gross bradykinesia. The examination was videoed with my permission. And the gait was not merely abnormal. The neurologist wrote:

“The gait is strikingly abnormal.”

Then something more specific. I walked with an appearance like foot drop, but it was not foot drop. The posture looked dystonic. The left leg kicked out. The arms did not swing. The whole body, in motion, was offering neurological information. And then, near the end of the examination findings, there were the feet. Pes planus. Hammertoes. That is almost the entire documented physical examination of them. The patient history had put the feet into the admission. The neurologist had seen two visible structural features. And then the record moved on. That is the part I did not understand until years later. Because nothing else in the admission stood still. On Monday, they were concerned enough about severe Parkinsonism with no obvious cause to organise a CT brain that evening. The scan was done. No acute intracranial pathology. Tuesday came. A chest X-ray was performed. Blood investigations were underway.

The search widened. Wednesday came. More blood was taken. Copper. Autoimmune testing. Paraneoplastic and neurological antibodies. Infection screening. Haematinics. Electrophoresis. The body was being interrogated in extraordinary biochemical detail. Thursday came. Friday came. The hospital was not idle. The case was being discussed as severe Parkinsonism in a thirty-nine-year-old man with no obvious cause. The age was strange. The symmetry was strange. The absence of a prominent tremor was notable. The possibility that symptoms had begun thirteen years earlier was notable. The clinicians were asking whether this could be an unusual inherited Parkinson's disease. They were thinking about synuclein. They were thinking about Parkinson-plus syndromes. They were thinking about glucocerebrosidase disease. They were thinking about toxic causes. They were thinking about disorders of metal accumulation. They were thinking about what future dopaminergic treatment might do to a man this young. They were thinking. That is precisely why the feet matter.

Because the question is not whether sophisticated medicine was happening. Sophisticated medicine was happening everywhere. The question is where it was being applied. I remember no one sitting down during those days and examining my feet as the unresolved neurological problem my GP had sent in. I remember no careful socks-off examination of what the toes did. I remember no attempt to reproduce the clawing Brennan had described. I remember no one asking me to show them what happened after I had been walking for a while. I remember no one following me through the ward because the gait deteriorated with use. I remember no one coming back on Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday or Friday and saying: show me the feet again. The medical record does not document such an investigation either. That distinction matters. There are things that can happen in a hospital and never make it into a note.

The absence of a note is not proof that no human eye ever looked downward. But this was an eight-day admission producing a detailed neurological history, a detailed examination, imaging, specialist discussion, allied-health involvement, extensive laboratory investigation and an unusually long explanatory letter. If a focused investigation of the feet occurred, comparable in seriousness to the investigation of the Parkinsonism, the record does not show it. What the record does show is strikingly sparse. The history I gave them. Corns. Callouses. Fallen arches. Longstanding treatment. Pain. Then what the neurologist saw. Pes planus. Hammertoes. And then almost nothing. The feet were present as history. They were present as appearance. They were not yet present as a serious neurological question. That is difficult to see if you read the admission quickly, because the rest of the medicine is so dense. Density creates an impression of completeness. A long history feels complete.

A long examination feels complete. A long differential feels complete. A long plan feels complete. But documentary density is not the same thing as investigative completeness. Sometimes the easiest way to miss an absence is to surround it with information. The weekend passed. I was still there. The hospital itself later explained that my young age and the possibility of a very early onset were reasons to keep me in as an inpatient until MRI could be obtained. The delay, the neurologist wrote, had in its own way suited everybody. It had allowed the team to get a lot done with me and explore a lot of questions. That sentence is true. They got a lot done. They explored a lot of questions. In fact, the Neurology Complex Case Meeting had already discussed my case on Tuesday 4 July, the day after I was admitted.

They had enough information to consider a recessively inherited Parkinson's disease. They had enough information to investigate whether I might have a disorder connected to Gaucher disease. They had enough information to consider the risks of starting a man my age on levodopa or a dopamine agonist. They had enough information to discuss my cognitive reserve. They had enough information to plan a very slow titration of medication after discharge. And still, from where I was standing, the oldest part of the problem had not been dealt with. The feet hurt. The toes were wrong. The walking was wrong. I had come in with all three joined together in Brennan's referral. Now Parkinsonism was being investigated at a level of sophistication I could not begin to follow, while the feet seemed to have become something that existed beside it. At first, I did not interpret that as a failure.

Why would I? I was in a neurology service. I had been told I had Parkinsonism. They were scanning me. Testing me. Discussing me. Explaining things to me. I assumed there was a sequence. First the serious neurological diagnosis. Then the rest. That assumption survived most of the admission. The feet had not been dealt with yet. Yet is a powerful word in a hospital. It makes delay feel temporary. It keeps trust intact. Monday became Tuesday. Not yet. Tuesday became Wednesday. Not yet. Wednesday became Thursday. Not yet. Thursday became Friday. Not yet. The weekend came. Not yet. Then came the second Monday. I was told I would be discharged the next morning. That changed the meaning of yet. There were four new medical students on the ward. I asked them to get the neurologist. He came. The curtain was closed around us. There were six people inside it.

The neurologist. The four students. And me. I had been there for a week. I had had the scans. I had given the history. I had been examined. I had been filmed walking. My case had become Parkinsonism. I was due to go home. And the feet were still the feet. So I put the problem back in the room. “Do you mean to tell me you're going to bring me in here, give me the full NCT, and send me out on bald tires?” That is how I remember saying it. An NCT is the Irish roadworthiness test. You can inspect the engine. You can test the lights. You can check the emissions. You can tell me the car has passed every sophisticated test available to you. But if the tires are bald, the car is not finished. That was the point. I was not disputing Parkinsonism.

I was not telling a neurologist how to diagnose a movement disorder. I was not rejecting the week of investigation. I was telling him that the thing I walked in on had not been dealt with. For most of the admission, there is still a charitable question available. Had the feet simply been forgotten? Hospitals are busy. Teams change. One problem becomes urgent and another waits. A referral is compressed. A history gets retold. A visible abnormality becomes background. Things fall out of working memory. That happens. But on the second Monday, I put the feet back in front of the person responsible for my neurological care. After that, forgetting was no longer enough to explain what happened next. The neurologist's reply, as I remember it, was:

“It's as easy for you to get a neurologist visit as it is for me.”

I have thought about that sentence many times. At the time, I did not turn it into an accusation. I was still inside the relationship. I still trusted him. I still believed the diagnosis was real. I still believed the hospital knew more than I did. And I still believed, perhaps most importantly, that an unresolved problem would remain unresolved until somebody resolved it. That is not what happened. The next morning, I was discharged. The record would not say that the feet had been forgotten. It would do something more consequential. It would explain them.

# Chapter Six — Completely Separate

The explanation did not begin with a discovery.

That is the first thing to understand. Nobody found a peripheral neuropathy. Nobody produced a test showing that the pain in my feet belonged to one disease and the rigidity, bradykinesia and abnormal gait belonged to another. Nobody established that the toe clawing my GP had written into his referral was unrelated to the Parkinsonism the hospital had just recognised. Nobody established that the older story in my lower limbs sat outside the neurological disease. The hospital had something much simpler than that. It had an unresolved problem at the point of discharge. And, by then, it also had an institutional problem. For a week, the feet could still have been an omission.

My GP had sent one trajectory into the hospital: abnormal feet, painful walking, an unusual gait, trouble initiating movement, increased tone, brisk reflexes, toe clawing, declining dexterity, deterioration at work. He had not sent Parkinson's disease with an unrelated footnote attached. He had sent a man whose body had been changing in more than one way and asked Neurology what connected it. Somewhere between that referral and the hospital's working version of me, the question narrowed. The hospital précis carried forward the material most recognisable as Parkinsonism: difficulty initiating movement, stiffness, increased tone, cogwheeling. The feet lost their place in the sentence. The hospital then did something genuinely impressive with what remained. It recognised severe Parkinsonism in a thirty-nine-year-old man. It examined my eye movements. It measured my rigidity. It watched my face. It watched my arms. It looked at my handwriting. It considered cognition. It ordered brain imaging.

It sent blood for rare causes. It considered Gaucher disease. It discussed recessive inheritance. It filmed me walking. It described the gait as "strikingly abnormal." It noticed that my left lower limb kicked out. It saw an appearance like foot drop and then corrected itself in capitals:

"NOT FOOT DROP."

It described the posture as suggestive of dystonia. It saw pes planus. It saw hammertoes. And across eight days of sophisticated neurological attention, the feet themselves remained almost untouched as a neurological question. That distinction matters. They were not invisible. They were not absent. They were under-investigated. For most of that week, there is still a version of events in which nobody has made a conscious decision about them at all. A referral can be compressed. A team can inherit the compressed version. A spectacular central diagnosis can dominate attention. A clinician can work hard on the problem he thinks he has been given. A patient can assume the hospital is carrying more of his history than it really is. A mistake can be made without anybody yet deciding to keep it. That is where the first seven days sit for me. The feet appear to have been overlooked.

Then I protested. I had been told I was going home the next morning. Four new medical students were on the ward. I asked them to get the neurologist. He came. The curtain closed around the six of us. "Do you mean to tell me you're going to bring me in here, give me the full NCT, and send me out on bald tires?" That sentence changed the epistemic position of everybody inside the curtain. I did not know it then. I know it now. Until that moment, the feet might have been lost somewhere in the machinery between referral, triage, admission, examination and working diagnosis. After that moment, they were no longer lost. The patient had found them for the hospital. There were four students standing there while I did it. The question was no longer how the feet had disappeared.

The question was what the hospital would do now that their disappearance had been exposed. That is the fork in this story. Not diagnosis or misdiagnosis. Not brilliance or stupidity. Repair or closure. Go back to the patient medically, or make the existing episode closable. If right were right, the protest should have changed the direction of the case. Someone should have gone back to the GP referral. Someone should have asked what exactly had entered the hospital and what exactly had been lost on the way through it. Someone should have taken my socks off with the referral in hand. What does "he claws his toes" mean here? What is happening when he walks? What is the relationship between the pain in the feet and the tension in the legs?

What did the GP mean when he put the feet, gait, tone, initiation difficulty and deterioration in the same referral? Does this dystonic-looking lower limb belong inside the movement disorder? If it does not, what evidence shows that? If peripheral neuropathy is a serious alternative, what evidence supports that? And if the clinician responsible for the case believes the movement disorder exceeds his own subspecialist expertise, who should own the unresolved question next? None of that required a genetic result. None of it required proving that every corn, callus, pain or deformity was neurological. It required only that the uncertainty stay attached to the people who had the means to investigate it. The hospital had those means. That is what makes what happened next so serious. The hospital did not lack science. It was using science everywhere. It could investigate my brain. It could investigate copper.

It could investigate autoimmune disease. It could investigate infection. It could investigate rare metabolic disease. It could discuss recessive Parkinsonism with colleagues. It could arrange a DaT scan. It could send me toward genetics. It could ask whether Gaucher disease belonged in the differential. If peripheral neuropathy was important enough to remove my feet from the Parkinsonian phenotype, the hospital could have added science to that "perhaps" too. It did not. Instead, the language changed. The wording is one of the most important passages in my medical history. The neurologist wrote: "The leg symptoms including corns and callouses and burning may have been completely separate or perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy perhaps in some respect related to the significant original alcohol intake? Not clear as this has improved." Read that slowly. The sentence looks modest.

"May have been."

"Perhaps."

"Perhaps."

"Not clear."

It has all the manners of uncertainty. But uncertainty is not neutral simply because it announces itself politely. You have to ask what has happened before the words "Not clear" arrive. There had been no burning. Not in my feet and not in my legs. I had not reported burning because burning was not part of my lived symptom history. That matters because "burning" is not merely a shorter version of something I had said. It is new sensory language. Before that sentence, the lower-limb story contained pain, corns, callouses, clawing toes, difficulty walking, tension, abnormal gait and dystonic-looking movement. Now it contained burning. Burning sits comfortably beside peripheral neuropathy. Toe clawing sits somewhere else. Dystonic posture sits somewhere else. A strikingly abnormal gait sits somewhere else. The record had not simply compressed my history. It had begun to transform the clinical object it was describing. Then it made another move.

The neurologist wrote that I had, in my own mind, "markedly divorced" the feelings of tension in my legs from the corns, callouses and pains in my feet. That sentence has followed me for years. I had made a distinction. Of course I had. The pain in my feet did not feel the same as the tension in my legs. One was pain. One was tension. I had spent years inside the body. I could tell the difference between sensations. But distinguishing two sensations is not the same thing as diagnosing two diseases. A patient saying these things feel different is not a patient saying these things have different neurological causes. The record crossed that distance for me. My discrimination became a divorce. That is a remarkable word. Not "distinguished." Not "described separately."

"Markedly divorced."

A medical partition was being written in language that made the partition appear to originate partly inside the patient's own mind. Years later, the sentence could be read back as evidence against me. He himself had divorced the symptoms. Except I had never made the medical proposition the sentence could now support. I had described my body. The record supplied the architecture. Then alcohol changed jobs. Alcohol had already been taken seriously in the admission. I drank too much. That belonged in the history. I had also told them why it had become useful to me physically: it relaxed a body I could not relax myself. The neurologist recorded that in unusually emphatic terms, writing that I had effectively been using alcohol as a "de-tensioning therapy." That placed alcohol downstream of the symptom. I had tension, and alcohol changed how the tension felt.

Now, in the foot synthesis, alcohol was moved upstream. Perhaps it had contributed to a secondary peripheral neuropathy. Perhaps that neuropathy explained the painful feet. The same fact had acquired a new causal function. Something I had used because of the body could now help explain what was wrong with the body. Again, "perhaps" did the work. Perhaps is a small word. It does not have to prove anything. That is its usefulness. No one had to establish peripheral neuropathy. No one had to establish an alcohol-related neuropathy. No one had to establish that the feet were neurologically separate. The proposition only had to become plausible enough to create another address for them. Once the feet had another address, the Parkinsonism could proceed without them. This is where the mechanism is laid down. Corns and callouses become corns, callouses and burning.

Foot pain and leg tension are gathered into "leg symptoms." My distinction between sensations becomes my having "markedly divorced" them. Alcohol moves from response to possible cause. Peripheral neuropathy appears without a corresponding neuropathy investigation. Then "Not clear" preserves the uncertainty after the feet have already been moved. The uncertainty does not disappear. It changes address. Before the admission, the question had been: What process could explain this evolving feet-gait-tone-movement presentation? After the synthesis, the question could become: What separate peripheral process might explain the residual foot problem? Those are not the same uncertainty. The first asks whether the model is complete. The second assumes enough separation to allow the model to continue. That difference is the whole book in miniature. And then there is the dystonic leg. The hospital itself had seen it. The gait was "strikingly abnormal." The footdrop-like appearance was described as suggestive of dystonic posture.

Later, in the DaT scan request, the walk was described as "almost dystonically unusual." That sentence is difficult to look away from. Because the dystonic lower limb was not too subtle to matter. It was being used. It helped make the Parkinsonism unusual. It helped make the case intellectually interesting. It sat inside reasoning toward an autosomal-recessive Parkinsonism. The department was already asking what kind of Parkinsonism a thirty-nine-year-old man with this strange, symmetrical, rigid presentation might have. And yet the same lower-limb territory was not allowed to force the feet back into the phenotype with equivalent force. The dystonic lower limb could make the Parkinsonism more unusual. It could not make the feet more neurological. That is not an absence of knowledge. It is a direction of use. Sophistication travelled upward into theory.

It did not travel downward into the painful, clawing feet attached to the man being theorised about. The formal reasoning toward recessive Parkinsonism could become increasingly refined while the lower-limb history became increasingly separate. The case became diagnostically richer and clinically narrower at the same time. And there was another fact inside the neurologist's own knowledge state. He would write of these conditions:

"I am not a movement disorders neurologist."

There is nothing shameful in that sentence. No doctor is every kind of specialist. The sentence becomes important because of what follows from knowing it. If right were right, "I am not a movement disorders neurologist" should have been a door. The protest should have been a door. The dystonic lower limb should have been a door. The suspicion of recessive Parkinsonism should have been a door. The unresolved feet should have moved toward the expertise capable of integrating them. Instead, the expertise boundary was acknowledged while the unresolved phenotype moved in the opposite direction. The Parkinsonism stayed inside increasingly sophisticated Neurology. The feet were sent toward Podiatry and a possible peripheral neuropathy. The clinical limitation was accommodated. The unresolved problem was divided.

The episode could close. Up to the protest, I cannot tell from the record whose mistake the missing feet were. Perhaps the referral had been compressed too early; perhaps the hospital version became dominant; perhaps the neurologist did not look deeply enough into what the GP had actually asked; perhaps nobody in the system was required to compare referral with investigation. The record does not require me to choose. After the protest, that uncertainty about responsibility mattered less. The failure had become visible. The hospital did not need to decide who was to blame before repairing it. It could have reopened the clinical question first and reconstructed the process later. Instead, closure continued.

That is where institutional responsibility changes character. The original omission may have been accidental. The decision made after it was exposed was not the same kind of event. No conspiracy is required. A system can protect an error simply by making it easier to continue with than to reopen. The patient still had painful, abnormal feet. The hospital had failed to carry the GP's integrated question through the admission. The neurologist had a dystonic-looking lower limb in an unusually young Parkinsonian presentation, a patient who had now challenged the completeness of the work-up, and an expertise boundary he himself recognised. The medically expansive response was to reopen, investigate, preserve the relationship, or move ownership toward greater expertise. The administratively convenient response was to divide the body into a neurological syndrome that could proceed and a peripheral problem that could go elsewhere. The surviving record shows the latter architecture.

The feet were not ruled out of the neurological story. They were moved out of it. With that move, the uncertainty acquired provenance. The patient knew his body and knew that the feet still hurt. The GP knew what he had referred. Later clinicians could know the finished record. The originating neurologist knew how the record had been made: that the feet had not been adequately investigated before discharge, that the patient had objected, that the gait looked dystonic, that peripheral neuropathy had not been demonstrated by an equivalent investigation, that "burning" had entered the synthesis, that my distinction between pain and tension had been rendered as a "marked divorce," and that he was not a movement-disorders neurologist. Everybody else could inherit the explanation. He carried its provenance.

That asymmetry would matter every time the feet returned. A later clinician could inherit "peripheral neuropathic pain" innocently. The author could not innocently inherit it from himself. Reopening the feet would also reopen 2017: what proved neuropathy, where burning came from, why a sensory distinction became a medical divorce, why alcohol acquired a causal role, why the dystonic lower limb informed the Parkinsonism without forcing a comparable investigation of the feet, and why an acknowledged expertise limit did not move the unresolved phenotype toward movement-disorders expertise. Everybody else would know what the record said. He would know why it said it. The plan made the split operational. Parkinsonism went forward through Neurology, imaging, genetics, medication and complex-case reasoning. The feet had Podiatry. The possible neuropathy had somewhere to live. The uncertainty was no longer a reason to stop. It had become a pathway. As per above.

If right were right, the hospital would have owned the uncertainty. It would have said, in substance: We have diagnosed severe young-onset Parkinsonism. We have also failed to resolve the longstanding painful, clawing, abnormal lower-limb phenotype with which this patient was referred. His gait looks dystonic. The relationship is not clear. It requires further neurological investigation and, where necessary, movement-disorders expertise. That would have been honest uncertainty. That would have protected the patient. That would have protected the GP's question. That would have protected the next doctor from mistaking a hypothesis for a history. That would have protected the neurologist too. Instead, the uncertainty was transferred. The hospital could close. The neurologist could disclaim the expertise he did not have without the unresolved phenotype being forced toward somebody who did. The record could say "perhaps." The record could say "Not clear." And I could carry the consequences.

A hospital can make a mistake and correct it. Something more dangerous happened here. The mistake was given a vocabulary. It was given a possible mechanism. It was given a possible cause. It was given words attributed to the patient. It was given an outpatient destination. It was given uncertainty respectable enough to survive. The gap in the investigation became a feature of the patient. For the first week, my feet appear to have been overlooked. After the protest, they could no longer be overlooked. From that point forward, they were handled by being placed somewhere else. Nothing had proved they belonged there. That was no longer necessary. The record had a category. The disease had been recognised. The syndrome had been divided. The uncertainty had acquired an author. And the division was ready to travel.

# Chapter Seven — As Per Above

The division was ready to travel. It did not have to become certain first. At the end of the long letter that followed my admission, after the scans, the genetics, the treatment discussion, the warnings, the explanations and the possibilities, the neurologist reached the heading **Plan/Recommendations**. The plan consisted of three words:

“As per above.”

They are easy words to pass over. In an ordinary medical letter they can look almost empty, a piece of administrative shorthand meaning that the plan has already been described. In my case they mattered because what stood “above” was no longer merely a description of uncertainty. Above was the new architecture of the case. Parkinsonism had its place. The feet had another. The Parkinsonism would move through Neurology, imaging, genetics and treatment. The feet could move through Podiatry and the possibility of peripheral neuropathy. None of that required the neuropathy to be proved. “Perhaps” had been enough to create the branch. “Not clear” had been enough to leave it open. “As per above” gave it a future. The first thing that happened to the neurological branch was that it became more sophisticated. The DaT scan request was written for a thirty-nine-year-old man with bilateral Parkinsonism, gross bradykinesia, little obvious tremor and a history that might extend back thirteen years. The neurologist described the presentation as atypical and then wrote something extraordinary about the walk:

“Walk is almost dystonically unusual.”

The next sentence was:

“We suspect an Autosomal recessive ‘Idiopathic’, Parkinsons.”

That was after the admission. After the eight days. After my protest. After the feet had been given their possible separate destination. The abnormal lower limb had not been forgotten. It was alive inside the neurological reasoning. It was useful there. The scan was abnormal, as expected, with marked bilateral loss of uptake in the basal ganglia. That did not tell the doctors which gene I had or explain every part of my body. What it did was strengthen the Parkinsonian branch. The question was no longer whether I had a serious Parkinsonian disorder. The question was what kind. The letter to my GP became increasingly ambitious. The neurologist explained why my age made the case unusual, why the symmetry was unusual, why the absence of a dominant family history mattered and why my cognition mattered. He described different levels of genetic possibility and then arrived at a specific question: whether the combination of very young onset, akinetic rigidity, lack of a dominant family history, marked symmetry and preserved cognition suggested a PRKN gene disorder — PARK2.

There was a striking omission from that list. The dystonic lower limb was not one of the explicit PRKN indicators set out in the paragraph. Neither were the feet. Neither was the toe clawing my GP had recorded. Neither was the painful distal history. The dystonic walk remained important enough to help make the overall Parkinsonism look recessive and unusual, but it did not travel into the formal PARK2 phenotype with the same force. The same lower limb now had two evidential lives. In one, it helped make the Parkinsonism genetically interesting. In the other, the painful feet attached to it were already living in a separate explanatory compartment. That separation then became productive in its own right. The neurologist did not stop at PARK2. He considered PARK6, PARK7 and even PARK9. In brackets beside PARK9 he wrote: “the neuropathy question”. That phrase is one of the clearest places in the record where the partition begins to think for itself.

A few weeks earlier my GP had sent one unresolved trajectory: painful feet, abnormal gait, toe clawing, increased tone, difficulty initiating movement, failing dexterity, deterioration. Now Neurology had two genetic questions. PARK2 belonged to the Parkinsonian core. PARK9 could belong to “the neuropathy question.” The original lower-limb presentation had been divided so successfully that each half could now generate its own sophisticated possibilities. The question joining them had disappeared between the branches. What if the painful feet, the clawing toes, the dystonic-looking lower limb and the Parkinsonian syndrome belonged, at least in part, to the same disease? Nobody had to answer that question in order for the new structure to work. The Parkinsonian branch had enough evidence to proceed. The peripheral branch had enough possibility to remain available. That was all the architecture required.

This is one of the strangest features of the record when I read it now. The medicine becomes more intelligent after the split. Not less. The vocabulary gets more specialised. The differential expands. Rare genes enter the discussion. Imaging is reviewed. Literature is enclosed. Colleagues are consulted. The Neurology Complex Case Meeting had already considered me on Tuesday 4 July, the day after admission. The neurologist writes at length about synuclein, inherited Parkinsonism, prognosis and treatment sensitivity. The case is not being neglected in any ordinary sense. It is being thought about intensely. But the thinking is now occurring inside the shape created at discharge. That is what “as per above” means in practice. The provisional synthesis is no longer merely retrospective — what we happened to think in July. It becomes prospective — the structure within which the next question will be asked. The neurologist also wrote something else in that long letter:

“I am not a movement disorders neurologist.”

By itself, that is professional candour. The case was unusual and he knew the limits of his own subspecialist expertise. He also wrote that genetic testing might be negative and that the science linking genes and phenotypes would probably become better with time. There is humility in that. There is also an obvious implication: if the case exceeded the expertise available to the treating neurologist, the unresolved parts of the phenotype had somewhere to go. The letter itself referred to colleagues in Dublin or the UK and said referral could occur if appropriate. One such colleague was Professor Daniel Healy in Dublin. The neurologist wrote to him for advice. He described me as thirty-nine, with symptoms that might have been present for thirteen years, and asked what genetic testing would be appropriate. He enclosed imaging. He said that if it would help, I was happy to be seen in Dublin, publicly or privately, for a once-off opinion. A video of my examination had been made with my consent and could be supplied. This was not a case trapped in a small clinic without access to expertise. The route outward existed. The intellectual network existed. The patient was available. The video existed. The question was being sent.

Professor Healy saw me on 12 September 2017. His first sentence back was direct:

“Thank you for asking me to see this man who I think probably has Park II and I would suggest testing that initially.”

If that was negative, he recommended moving to a panel. Two months after I had entered hospital, a second neurologist was not merely agreeing that I had Parkinsonism. He was pointing specifically at PARK2. He thought the onset had probably been somewhere between my late twenties and early thirties. He noted very few non-motor symptoms. He recorded a dramatic improvement in my motor symptoms on procyclidine and rasagiline. He explained that people with PARK2 could often manage for long periods with relatively low doses of dopamine replacement, while becoming dyskinetic if over-treated. For the Parkinsonian branch, this was sophisticated, careful medicine. The case had gone from an urgent GP referral to severe young-onset Parkinsonism, to recessive genetic reasoning, to a specialist opinion that I probably had PARK2. The uncertainty was narrowing in an intellectually coherent direction.

The feet did not travel with it.

That is the point to hold onto. Not because Professor Healy should somehow have solved every foot symptom in one consultation, and not because PARK2 by itself would have proved that every corn, callus or pain had a neurological cause. Structural pathology was real. Mixed pathology was always possible. The important fact is simpler. As the Parkinsonian explanation became more specific, the unresolved lower-limb phenotype did not become more integrated. It remained on the other side of the border created at discharge.

The border was now doing work.

On one side: very young onset, symmetry, akinetic rigidity, DaT scan abnormality, autosomal-recessive Parkinsonism, PRKN/PARK2, specialist genetics advice. On the other: the feet, Podiatry, possible peripheral neuropathy, possible alcohol contribution, the neuropathy question. The same body generated both tracks. The record allowed them to develop independently. I did not experience any of this as a schism. I experienced it as care. I had a diagnosis. I had appointments. I had a neurologist writing long letters because my situation was complicated. I was sent for specialist opinion. There was talk of genetics and treatment strategy. I had copies of correspondence. The doctors were plainly taking me seriously. If anything, the increasingly elaborate reasoning strengthened my trust. A rare possibility sounds like attention. A second opinion sounds like thoroughness. A long letter sounds like somebody has thought about you. And, crucially, I was beginning to feel better.

That matters because the body can settle arguments the patient does not yet know are happening. I was not sitting at home comparing the GP referral with the discharge synthesis and asking why toe clawing had disappeared from one document while dystonia remained useful in another. I was learning what Parkinsonism meant. I was trying medications. I was trying to get back to work. I wanted to move. The treatment plan had been cautious. The neurologists were reluctant to use levodopa immediately because I was so young and because of the concern that people with some young-onset forms of Parkinsonism could develop dyskinesias earlier. So they tried other symptomatic approaches first. Procyclidine was one of them. Rasagiline was another. The plan was gradual. They were deliberately watching what happened. What happened was not subtle. Professor Healy would describe the improvement as “dramatic.” I had my own language for it. At first, it did not feel like an argument about disease classification. It felt like being given pieces of my body back.

The records were becoming more complicated. My life was about to become simpler.

# Chapter Eight — Melted Snowman

There are ways of being ill that become ordinary simply because they last long enough. Tension had been part of my life for years. I knew it in bed, in my legs, in the effort of starting to move, in the way walking deteriorated as distance accumulated. I knew what it was like to think about movement before moving. I knew what it was like to stand up and wait for the body to join in. I knew what it was like to drink at night and feel some of that resistance soften. I did not think of myself as conducting experiments. I was just living inside whatever my body had become. After the hospital, that began to change. The treatment plan was cautious because I was young. The neurologists did not want to rush toward levodopa or a dopamine agonist if they could get useful control another way. Rasagiline came in. Procyclidine came in gradually. Clonazepam was there at night for the tension. The names were new to me. What mattered was what they did.

One evening I was having a pint when the tension stopped.

That is the memory I keep. Not a clinic. Not a scan. Not somebody explaining basal ganglia to me. A pint in front of me and my body becoming different. The release was beautiful. I loosened out, as I later described it, like a melted snowman. Something I had been carrying for so long that it had become part of the weather inside me suddenly gave way. I remember thinking: “God, is this how good everybody else feels?” The question was half joke, half revelation. Until then I had known my own body only from inside it. There was no control version of me walking around beside me showing what normal ease felt like. If every movement contains resistance for long enough, resistance becomes the unit by which you measure everything. Then, suddenly, it was not there in the same way. I could feel the difference because the difference was enormous.

That was when treatment ceased to be an abstract medical proposition. The doctors had not merely named something. They had changed something I could feel. This matters to the story of trust. Looking back, it would be easy to imagine that I should have been auditing everything. I had protested about the feet. The feet had been sent toward Podiatry. A possible neuropathy had appeared in the record. The Parkinsonian side of the case had moved into genetics and specialist opinion. Why did I not keep demanding that somebody put all of it back together?

Because I was getting better.

That is the simplest answer.

I had spent years adapting to a body that was becoming harder to operate. Then medicine intervened and I could move. The neurologist who had told me I had Parkinsonism had given me a treatment plan that was producing obvious bodily benefit. Professor Healy later described the motor improvement on procyclidine and rasagiline as dramatic. I did not need his adjective. I knew what dramatic felt like. In October 2017, during a planned break from procyclidine, I wrote down what the medication had given me. “My walk is back together.” “My writing is good.” “I can stand up straight.” “My legs are loose and limber.” I could stand on a moving bus without the old calculation. I could lift my bicycle without falling over it. I could jump. Run. Hop. Skip. Ordinary verbs had become evidence. I called the period “living in a miracle, literally.”

There is something almost embarrassing about that sentence when I read it now, not because it was exaggerated, but because it was exact. I was not thinking like a future complainant. I was not suspicious. I was relieved. My body had become possible again. The planned withdrawal was useful because it showed the treatment was doing real work. Four days into the break I wrote that I could feel “the tension building up,” that “my feet are restless” and “my walk is falling apart.” At the same time my eyesight, which had been affected by the medication, was improving. The trade-off was visible in real time. Off the drug, one problem improved and another returned. That email is important now because it catches me before later theories had hardened. I was simply reporting what happened. Tension returned. The feet became restless. The walk deteriorated. I did not know what mechanism connected those things. I was not trying to prove that the feet belonged to Parkinsonism. I was telling somebody what my body did when the medication was withdrawn.

At the time, though, the meaning I took from it was reassuring rather than destabilising. The medication worked. That was enough. If the drug had been intended for Parkinsonian symptoms and my body improved under it, then the broad medical story felt confirmed. The fact that my feet improved as part of that bodily change did not make me interrogate the border between Parkinsonism and the separate foot problem. It made me think the doctors had found something useful. This is one of the strange privileges of effective treatment: it can make unanswered questions feel less urgent. Pain that has occupied your attention stops occupying it. Walking that has required planning becomes ordinary. You do not necessarily ask which paragraph of the discharge letter has been vindicated. You go outside.

By late October I was telling work that, apart from the problem with my eyesight, I was physically very much improved. “You wouldn’t believe there was ever much wrong with me,” I wrote. The joy of being able to move and function normally, I said, was “the equivalent of living in a miracle.” That was not rhetoric intended for a doctor. It was a description sent into ordinary life. I wanted to work again. I wanted to function. I wanted the body to recede from consciousness. The eyesight problem complicated the medication, and doses had to be adjusted. I was taken off procyclidine for a period and then put back on at a lower dose. The lower dose did not control everything, so the plan was adjusted again. That is what good treatment often looks like from the inside: not a clean cure, but negotiation. Benefit, side effect, reduction, return of symptoms, adjustment, another attempt. The important thing was that there was something to negotiate with. Before the hospital, deterioration had felt like a one-way process. Now there were levers.

That altered my relationship with Neurology profoundly. I did not trust because every question had been answered. I trusted because something they had done had changed the terms on which I lived in my own body. The distinction is important. A patient can carry unresolved pathology and still have very good reasons to believe in the people treating him. In fact, treatment benefit can make trust stronger precisely when the explanatory model remains incomplete. The feet are the clearest example. I had gone into hospital with years of painful feet and a walk that was disintegrating. I had come close to discharge believing the feet had not been properly dealt with and had said so. Then, in the weeks that followed, neurological treatment helped me walk properly again. The practical loop closed before the intellectual one did. Whatever the record said about Podiatry, neuropathy, corns, callouses or alcohol, my lived experience was simpler: the neurologists had treated me and my body had improved, including the part of it I had been most worried about. That was good enough for me.

The phrase matters because it explains years.

“That’s good enough for me.”

Not: every mechanism has been proved. Not: every part of the history has been reconciled. Not: I now understand why my feet hurt for years. Good enough meant that I could move. It meant I could imagine returning to work. It meant the future was larger than it had looked in June. It meant the people who had told me I had Parkinsonism had also found something that made me feel dramatically better. There is another irony in the old record. Alcohol had already been given two different meanings. In the admission history it was recognised as something I had used as a de-tensioning treatment. In the separate-foot synthesis it became a possible contributor to a neuropathy. But in my actual life the pint in front of me on the evening I loosened out was just a pint. I was not thinking about causal models. I was sitting there noticing that the body was releasing.

That distinction between the record and the evening matters. Clinical records necessarily convert life into categories. Alcohol intake. Medication response. Motor symptoms. Adverse effects. But relief does not arrive in categories. It arrives as the shoulders dropping, the legs softening, the walk becoming available, the body no longer demanding to be managed every second. For years I had been doing things around the illness. Choosing shoes around it. Choosing distance around it. Using drink around it. Thinking about work around it. Thinking about standing up around it. Suddenly the body gave some space back. The later record would accumulate far more complicated arguments about what belonged where. I did not know any of that. What I knew was that a pill had changed my gait. A pill had changed my writing. A pill had changed the tension in my legs. Taking it away changed them back. Putting treatment back into the system improved me again.

The body was producing evidence. I was experiencing relief. Those are not the same thing. Evidence asks to be integrated. Relief asks to be lived. I chose, quite reasonably, to live it. That is why the next years matter so much. They were not eight years of sitting inside a medical mistake waiting for the truth to reveal itself. They contained work, darts, walking, travel, drinking, stopping drinking, ordinary routines, jokes, friendships and long stretches in which Parkinson’s was something I had rather than the only thing I was. The treatment gave me back enough physical confidence that I stopped measuring every pavement as a threat. By December I was back at work. I would later work in shipping. I played darts. I walked. There were still problems and there would be more, but the essential fact of that period was expansion. Before diagnosis the body had been narrowing my life. After treatment, for a while, life widened again.

That widening is why the medical story could settle so deeply inside me. The Parkinsonism diagnosis did not merely exist on paper. It had a treatment consequence I could feel. The people caring for me did not merely speak with authority. Their interventions worked. The distinction between the Parkinsonian body and the separately explained feet therefore did not feel dangerous. It felt administrative. Whatever the categories were, the person was doing better. Only much later would I understand how much significance could hide inside a category while the patient was getting on with his life. At the time I was not thinking about “perhaps.” I was not thinking about “Not clear.” I was not thinking about PRKN, PARK9 or the provenance of uncertainty. I was thinking about being able to stand straight. About getting onto a bus. About carrying things. About whether the eyesight would settle. About getting back to work.

And above all, I was thinking about walking. For years walking had been something I managed. Soon it would become something else entirely. I would become unintimidated by it.

# Chapter Nine — Unintimidated by Walking

For a while, walking stopped being the problem. That sentence would have sounded absurd to me a few months earlier. Before the hospital, walking had become a negotiation with distance. I knew what it was to leave somewhere feeling almost normal and then feel the body deteriorate under me as the journey went on. I knew what it was to stand up and prepare myself for the first step, to think about balance, to calculate a bus before I reached it, to be aware of pavements in a way nobody should have to be aware of pavements. Walking had become an activity with a cost attached to it. Then treatment changed the price.

I went back to work in December. I later moved into shipping and stayed there until the following September. I do not remember that period in the kind of detail a novelist would like. There is no single morning I can recover, no perfect scene of me walking through a door and thinking, I am back. Memory is not obliged to preserve the moments that would make the cleanest chapter. What remains is the fact of return. I was working again. I was moving again. I could stand. I could get from one place to another without feeling that every journey might become a small emergency. That mattered more to me than the paperwork behind it. Parkinson's was real. I accepted that. I took the medication. I went to appointments. I learned the names of drugs and side effects and the small routines that grow around long-term illness. But I was not spending my days thinking about disease architecture. I was thinking about ordinary things. Work. Darts. Pints. People. Money. Sleep. Whether I was going out. Whether I would play well. Whether I could be bothered doing something. The illness occupied part of my life, but it did not occupy all of it.

That is an important distinction. There are periods in chronic illness when a person is not cured, not even close to cured, but is no longer living as a patient every hour of the day. The body becomes sufficiently reliable that attention can go elsewhere. Mine did. I played darts. I had shoulder trouble. I adapted. For months I played left-handed because the right shoulder would not cooperate. “I refused to let God take my darts from me, literally.” That sentence contains more of me than any clinical summary could. It was not courage in the grand sense. It was obstinacy. If one arm would not do it, I would use the other one. The board was still there. Walking was different. Walking had no substitute. You cannot throw with your other legs. When walking had been bad, it had governed everything around it. Where I went. How long I stayed. What shoes I wore. Whether I wanted to stand. Whether I would have to get home. Improvement therefore did something larger than make me more mobile. It removed a layer of calculation from ordinary life.

By the end of 2019 another change came. I stopped drinking on 30 December. I chose the date deliberately. If I waited until New Year's Eve, then it became a resolution, and resolutions are things people break. The thirtieth was simply a day. So I stopped. The night routine changed with it. For years drink had been part of the way I came down from the day. It had been social, habitual, physical, medicinal in my own unsophisticated way. If I could not sleep after stopping, I walked. I walked and walked. Miles, sometimes. There is something almost comic about that when I think back to the man who had once been timing his movement against the braking of a bus so that the vehicle would help propel him out of the seat. Now, at night, if I was restless, I could put on shoes and go.

I was not checking whether I could make it home. I knew I could. That is what I mean when I say I was unintimidated by walking. It is difficult to explain how large a freedom that is unless walking has frightened you. Most people do not negotiate with it. They get up. They leave. They go somewhere. The body performs the transport quietly. Once that quietness is lost, its return can feel enormous. Walking became boring again, and boring was wonderful. I booked trips. I went away. The exact chronology of the holidays is something I can now check in records, but the bodily memory is simpler than the itinerary. A good day away could begin around half eight with breakfast, then the pool, a shower, shorts, a T-shirt, sandals. I could swim, go to the beach, play a game, head into town, shop, sit somewhere with an ice cream, go back for another swim, sleep for a while, have dinner, maybe go for a drink, then walk home.

Nothing in that list is dramatic. That is why I value it. Breakfast. Pool. Beach. Town. Ice cream. Dinner. Walk home. A life is built largely from things that would never enter a medical record because they are too ordinary to deserve a heading. Yet those are the things illness takes first and treatment gives back most convincingly. A consultant can write that gait has improved. A person experiences the improvement as being able to stay out after dinner without wondering whether the road back to the hotel will defeat him. I was still solitary. Being away was not some grand reinvention in which I became a different man and collected strangers around me. I liked being myself somewhere else. That was enough. There is a particular pleasure in being alone without being trapped. You can walk to town because you want to. You can turn around because you want to. You can sit by yourself because you choose to rather than because the body has made the choice for you.

When treatment was working, I did not feel ill in the way people imagine illness. “You don't feel ill when you're practicing. Your condition is somewhat treated. You're not a patient.” That is how I remember it. Parkinson's existed. Medication existed. Appointments existed. But most of the day could belong to something else. This is also why the later history cannot be told as though I spent every year after 2017 suspicious of what had happened in the hospital. I did not. The doctors had named something real. The treatment was doing real work. My life had expanded. If there was a conceptual problem buried in the way the feet had been classified, I was not living at the level of conceptual problems. I was living at the level of whether I could walk into town. And I could. There were clues, of course, if one already knew how to interpret them. The body did not become perfectly tidy simply because I felt better. The toes still existed. The feet still had their own history. There were supports. There were episodes. There were things that would later matter. But at the time those details did not announce themselves as contradictions. They were inconveniences inside a larger improvement.

That is one of the easiest mistakes to make when reading a life backwards. Once you know the later problem, every earlier detail starts glowing. The person living the day does not see the glow. He sees the day. I saw good days. I saw work returning. Darts returning. Distance returning. Travel returning. I saw the possibility that the body might still allow me a life that was recognisably mine. The medical record could say what it liked about categories. I was judging medicine by a simpler instrument: could I live? For a while, the answer was yes. And that period matters because it explains why trust was not passive. It was reinforced repeatedly by function. I was not merely deferring to authority. I was walking evidence of benefit. I could move further. I could do more. I could occupy space without calculating the exit. The diagnosis and the treatment had become entangled with relief, and relief is persuasive.

If somebody had asked me then whether Neurology understood me, I would not have answered by comparing the GP referral with the discharge letter. I had not done that work. I would have answered from the body. I was better. My walk was better. My life was bigger. That was the truth available to me. The other truth was still there, but it had not yet become visible as a story. It was waiting in the record, and in the feet, and in the small things that kept returning.

# Chapter Ten — The Doctor Who Knew How to Look

My right shoulder went in 2019 and, because darts was darts, I spent the better part of a year playing left-handed.

“I refused to let God take my darts from me, literally.”

That is how I put it now, but the important thing is that I did not stop. I changed hands. I adapted. The shoulder was painful enough to alter what I could do, but it did not become a reason to surrender the board. It became another problem to work around until somebody could tell me what it was. This time, Neurology looked. The difference is visible in the record almost immediately. The neurologist did not begin with a label and work backwards. He began with the pain. Where did it start? Where did it travel? What made it worse? What made it disappear? He followed it from the back of the shoulder blade through the shoulder and armpit and down the arm. He tested power. He found weakness in the triceps. He checked wrist and finger extension, reflexes and tone. He tried to distinguish weakness caused by pain from weakness that reproduced as a neurological finding.

Then the possibilities widened. It might be the shoulder itself. It might be a cervical nerve root. It might be the radial nerve. It might be the posterior cord of the brachial plexus. It might be brachial neuritis. It might be structural injury. It might, less comfortably, be malignancy. My smoking history mattered, but it did not get to dictate the answer. The anatomy had to make sense. After working through all of that, he wrote a sentence I have come to admire:

“I genuinely do not know what the cause is here.”

There is a form of uncertainty that is simply absence. Nobody has looked closely enough, so nobody knows. This was not that. This uncertainty had been earned. It came after examination, localisation, competing explanations and an attempt to work out what fitted and what did not. Most importantly, not knowing did not end the inquiry. It generated the next step. He wanted imaging. I was worried I would not be able to stay still in an MRI because of pain, restlessness and movement. That practical difficulty did not become a reason to leave the question alone. He thought around it, considered what other imaging could and could not show, and ultimately arranged MRI under general anaesthesia. That was medicine behaving the way I would have expected medicine to behave. The uncertainty stayed attached to the problem until there was more evidence.

The shoulder also shows how carefully he could reason about treatment response. Gabapentin changed the pain somewhat. It would have been easy to say that because a drug often used for nerve pain helped, the problem must therefore be neuropathic. He did not do that. He explained that different mechanisms could respond to the same drug. A shoulder problem, a nerve-root problem, a plexus problem or something else could all change with treatment. Response was evidence. It was not proof. He handled steroids the same way. If steroids helped, that still would not tell him automatically what the diagnosis was. Structural, inflammatory and compressive problems might all improve. Again, the response could inform the question without being allowed to answer it by itself. The distinction is simple and important: a treatment can change a symptom without proving why the symptom existed. He knew that.

Alcohol gives the shoulder episode another kind of clarity. Radial nerve problems can be associated with alcohol, so alcohol was a possible fact in the history. But he did not stop there. He tested the idea against the anatomy. The triceps weakness did not fit the ordinary mechanism of an alcohol-associated radial neuropathy because the relevant branches arose too high. The explanation failed the anatomical test, so he rejected it. Later he was blunter still: reducing alcohol, he wrote, was not going to help the shoulder and was not going to help the Parkinson's. That is a revealing piece of clinical reasoning. History suggested a convenient explanation. Anatomy did not support it. The explanation was discarded. The shoulder record is full of that kind of work. He considered my fear of cancer without letting fear become diagnosis. He reasoned through what an apical lung tumour would have to do to produce the observed weakness. He considered the limits of the chest X-ray. He did not dismiss the concern, but he did not inflate it either. He explained.

There is also a small documentary detail that matters to me now. In the shoulder correspondence he explicitly described the value of writing things down in real time so that I would have the letter at home and we would share an understanding of what he meant. He did not want there to be any concern about “double meaning.” That is an admirable instinct. A doctor says what he thinks, says what he does not know, explains what the evidence can and cannot prove, and gives the patient the same account so that both people are standing in roughly the same informational place. I did not read the shoulder episode in 2019 as a lesson in medical method. I was trying to get my arm back. I was playing darts left-handed and waiting for somebody to work out what had gone wrong. The larger comparison came much later, when I had the records in front of me.

By then I had already seen another uncertainty handled very differently. Two years earlier, the feet had entered hospital inside a neurological presentation that already contained abnormal gait, increased tone, toe clawing and difficulty moving. Neurology itself had described the lower limb as dystonic-looking. I had complained before discharge that the feet had not been dealt with. The final account had nevertheless made them capable of being “completely separate,” perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy, “Not clear.” Then treatment supplied another piece of information. Procyclidine changed my whole motor state. When it was withdrawn, I wrote contemporaneously that the tension was building, my feet were restless and my walk was falling apart. The neurologist later recorded that the first thing to change, and to change fairly rapidly, was the return of the outside-left-foot phenomenon, together with broader worsening and an inability to relax. That did not prove that every painful lesion in my feet was neurological. It did not prove that every corn, callus or structural problem belonged to Parkinsonism. It did something narrower. It showed that a foot phenomenon changed with neurological treatment state.

The shoulder record tells us exactly how such evidence could have been handled. Interesting. What exactly is changing? Where does it localise? What does the treatment response tell us? What does it not tell us? Which competing mechanisms remain? What investigation could discriminate between them? That was how the shoulder was approached. No certainty was required before curiosity began. That is what makes the comparison difficult to escape. The same neurologist knew how to map an uncertain painful problem, how to test explanations against anatomy, how to refuse easy causal stories, how to understand that drug response is not diagnosis, how to adapt investigations to the patient, how to say plainly that he did not know, and how to use that uncertainty as a reason to keep looking. The shoulder was complex. Complexity did not paralyse him. The feet were complex too. The difference was not whether he possessed the habits required to investigate uncertainty. The record shows that he did.

The difference was what the uncertainty was allowed to do. With the shoulder, uncertainty produced examination, localisation, competing hypotheses, imaging and follow-through. With the feet, uncertainty had already been given another job. It had become capable of supporting separation. That is why the shoulder matters so much to me now. It is not an external standard imposed years later. I do not need to invent an ideal neurologist and ask why my doctor failed to resemble him. The comparator is already inside my own notes. He supplied it himself. He showed what his medicine looked like when he decided a painful problem remained open. He could look closely. He could reject a convenient explanation. He could say, without embarrassment, that he did not know. And when he did not know, he could go looking. The shoulder uncertainty produced investigation. The feet uncertainty produced architecture.

# Chapter Eleven — The Toe That Would Not Stay Omitted

The shoulder showed that my neurologist knew how to look. The toes show what happened when something kept asking to be looked at and never quite became a neurological question. They were there before I reached the hospital. Aaron Brennan had put them in the referral plainly enough. At night, I could not relax my muscles and “he claws his toes.” There was nothing obscure about the phrase. The toes were doing something. They were not merely shaped a certain way. They were clawing. By the time the hospital wrote its own version of the referral, that detail had gone. The précis carried the movement difficulty, the increased tone, the stiffness and the cogwheeling, but the toe clawing did not travel with them. During the admission the feet reappeared visually as pes planus and hammertoes. Those were true observations, but they were different kinds of observation. A hammertoe is a shape. Toe clawing is behaviour. One tells you what the foot looks like. The other tells you what it does.

That distinction mattered because the lower limb was already behaving strangely enough for Neurology to call the gait “strikingly abnormal” and the posture somewhat suggestive of dystonia. Later, in the DaT-scan request, the walk would be described as “almost dystonically unusual.” Yet the toes never seem to have received the same interrogative attention. Were they fixed? Did they contract? Did they release? Did walking make them worse? Did rest make them better? Did medication alter them? Were the structural changes primary, secondary to years of abnormal loading, neurologically driven, or some mixture of all three? The surviving neurological record does not show that inquiry being built. Podiatry, naturally, saw the toes. Podiatry treated what was in front of it. Supports were made. Ottoforms appeared. By 2018 Neurology itself recorded: “Toe supports on both feet are a help.” That is a small sentence, but it tells us quite a lot. There was a bilateral toe problem. It was persistent enough to require physical supports. The supports helped enough for that fact to reach the neurologist and be written down. The toes had therefore crossed back into Neurology as information.

What they did not acquire was a history. There is no visible sequence in the neurological record saying: this is the same clawing Brennan described before admission; this is how it behaves now; this is whether it changes with Parkinsonian state; this is whether it resembles dystonia; this is how it relates to the dystonic-looking lower limb; this is what Podiatry has changed and what it has not. The information arrives. The relationship does not. For me, at the time, there was nothing mysterious about any of this. I had bad feet. People worked on them. Things sometimes helped. I was walking better. Parkinson's treatment was helping me. I had no reason to sit at home constructing a taxonomy of toe movement. If a support made my toes more comfortable, I used the support. If I could play darts, I played darts.

Darts is where the toes become almost absurdly difficult to ignore. A dart throw looks static if you watch only the arm. It is not. You set your feet. You load your weight. You hold the stance. You throw. You reset. Then you walk to the board, retrieve the darts, turn, walk back and start again. Hundreds of tiny repetitions depend on the feet staying beneath you without demanding attention. Mine had been demanding attention for years. Long before Parkinsonism was named, a darts night could end with my body folded forward, walking badly, still wanting another throw. The stance, the stop-start movement, the turning, the repeated return to the oche all asked the same lower limbs to behave over and over again. The toes belonged inside that experience whether medicine gave them a name or not. Years later, the neurological record contains another phrase:

“the toe curling.”

The definite article is almost comic. Not *toe curling has appeared*. Not *he now reports a new phenomenon*. Not *we should characterise this movement*. Just *the toe curling*. By then it is familiar. That is what catches my eye now. A phenomenon can become familiar without ever acquiring a proper genealogy. Everybody can know that it happens. Everybody can treat around it. Everybody can mention it. Yet the record can still fail to ask the most basic historical question: is this the same thing that was present at the beginning? The language had already changed once. Brennan had written that I clawed my toes. The admission had seen hammertoes. Podiatry had made supports. Neurology had recorded that the supports helped. Later there was “the toe curling.” Those phrases sit years apart, in different services and different contexts. Read separately, each is small. Put them in order and they begin to look like one stubborn witness moving through the file.

The toe keeps returning. It returns structurally. It returns functionally. It returns in Podiatry. It returns in Neurology. It returns in darts. It returns even after the feet have been moved into their own explanatory compartment. And still it does not force the original relationship back open. That is what makes the toe useful now. Not because a curled toe proves dystonia. It does not. Not because every hammertoe is neurological. It is not. Not because structural disease was imaginary. It was real. The toe matters because it was exactly the kind of recurring, dynamic, lower-limb phenomenon that should have remained capable of changing the question. Instead, it kept being recorded locally. Podiatry could see a deformity and help mechanically. Neurology could know the supports helped. I could feel the curling while throwing darts. Each fact could be true. The problem was that truth kept arriving in pieces.

This was the same difficulty that had begun in hospital. The facts survived. Their relationships travelled badly. The toe is where that becomes almost impossible to miss because the phenomenon is so small. There is nowhere for rhetoric to hide. A toe either keeps appearing in the history or it does not. Mine did. What did not appear with equal persistence was curiosity about what connected the appearances. By the time the phrase “the toe curling” entered the record, the movement had become ordinary enough to be referred to casually. That casualness is important. It means the phenomenon was not a surprise. It belonged to the known patient. Yet the surviving record still does not show a comparable neurological reconstruction of it from the original clawing onward. I think about that now whenever I see how medicine distributes a body across specialties. Podiatry was not wrong to treat the toes. Mechanical help was still help. Structural pathology did not cease to exist because Parkinsonism existed. The mistake is to think that a destination is an explanation.

A toe support can help a toe without telling you why the toe needed supporting. A podiatry appointment can improve a foot without answering whether the movement disorder helped create the problem. A structural description can be correct without proving structural independence. The record did not need any of those distinctions to disappear completely. It only needed them never to be reunited. That is how the toe could remain present and still be absent from the neurological story that mattered. It had been in the GP's referral. It had been lost in compression. It had been seen as structure in hospital. It had been treated in Podiatry. Its supports had been reported back to Neurology. It later curled during darts. It became familiar enough to acquire the definite article. What it never acquired, at least in the surviving record, was the sustained question it had earned at the beginning.

The toes were not absent.

They were under-asked.

# Chapter Twelve — The Words That Changed the Feet

The toes had kept returning. The words had been changing around them. That is easier to see now because I can put the documents beside one another. At the time I could not. I had a body, a diagnosis, appointments, shoes, supports, medication and whatever language the person in front of me used. I did not go home from Neurology and compare nouns. But nouns matter. In the GP referral, the feet belonged inside a moving body. There was the abnormal gait, the stiffness, the increased tone, the difficulty initiating movement, the loss of dexterity and the fact that I clawed my toes. The feet were not a separate chapter. They were part of the question. Inside the hospital record the language began to reorganise them. By discharge, the synthesis referred to “the leg symptoms including corns and callouses and burning,” which “may have been completely separate,” or perhaps represented “a secondary peripheral neuropathy,” possibly related in some respect to alcohol. Then came the neatest phrase of all: “Not clear.”

There are several changes packed into those few lines. Feet become leg symptoms. Pain acquires burning. My distinction between foot pain and muscular tension is rewritten as evidence that I had “markedly divorced” one from the other. A possible peripheral neuropathy appears. Alcohol is given a possible causal role. None of those phrases has to become certain. They only have to create somewhere else for the feet to belong. The addition of burning matters because there was no burning before or during that admission, and there would be no burning until after Sinemet entered my life in 2021. I had not reported burning because there was no burning to report. I had corns. I had callouses. I had pain. I had tension. I had described different sensations differently. “Burning” arrived in the synthesis years before it arrived in my body, and once it arrived the phenotype sounded more neuropathic than it had before. The description and the explanation began to help one another. That is the first stage of what happened to the feet in language. The clinical object changed while being described.

It did not happen in one dramatic jump. It happened by degrees. First there were feet. Then leg symptoms. Then burning. Then a possible peripheral neuropathy. Then, in the genetics discussion, PARK9 appeared as “the neuropathy question.” By that point neuropathy had become productive enough to generate its own branch of thought. The Parkinsonian side could produce PARK2. The residual side could produce PARK9. A single lower-limb history had become capable of asking two separate genetic questions. None of that required anybody to write that neuropathy had been proven. That is the important part. A provisional category can become durable without ever becoming definitive. It only needs to be useful enough to organise what comes next. I did not experience any of this as language at the time. I experienced it as ordinary adaptation. If Podiatry made a support, I wore the support. If a runner hurt, I found a softer runner. If I needed insoles and plasters before I trusted a new pair of shoes, I bought them. If the toes curled during darts, I played around them. If a treatment helped, I took the help.

The record, meanwhile, needed names.

That is what records do. They compress. They classify. They make bodies portable. A doctor who has never met you cannot inherit your feet. They inherit the words used for your feet. That is why apparently small changes matter. Painful feet are not the same documentary object as leg pain. Leg pain is not the same documentary object as burning. Burning is not the same documentary object as neuropathic pain. And “perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy” is not the same documentary object as “peripheral neuropathic pain symptomatologies.” Those phrases may all refer to some overlapping piece of the same lived experience, but they do not ask the same question. The earliest formulation was uncertain about the category itself. Perhaps this was a peripheral neuropathy. Perhaps it was completely separate. Not clear. Years later, the language had matured. The neurologist would write of “whatever the underlying cause for the peripheral neuropathic pain symptomatologies that Liam has.”

At first glance that sounds cautious too. It contains “whatever the underlying cause.” It acknowledges uncertainty. But it is a different kind of uncertainty. In 2017 the question was: is peripheral neuropathy the right explanation for these symptoms? Later the question had become: what is the underlying cause of these peripheral neuropathic pain symptoms?

The uncertainty had moved.

The category had stayed.

That is one of the most important things I discovered when I finally began reading the record longitudinally. Uncertainty does not always disappear when a case settles. Sometimes it changes address. The word *perhaps* can vanish while the structure created by the *perhaps* remains. No single later note has to announce that this has happened. There is no ceremony. Nobody stamps the file and writes: HYPOTHESIS NOW PROMOTED TO HISTORY. The shift occurs because future sentences begin from a different starting point. Once the feet are spoken of as neuropathic, later questions naturally become questions about neuropathic pain. Which drug? Which dose? Which cause? Which rare condition? Which response? The earlier question — whether the feet belonged in that compartment in the first place — becomes harder to see because it is no longer built into the grammar.

The grammar does the work quietly.

This is not merely a philosophical point about medical language. It changes what can happen next. A person with painful feet, toe clawing, dystonic-looking gait and Parkinsonism invites one kind of inquiry. A person with Parkinson's disease and separate peripheral neuropathic pain invites another. The body may be the same body. The future of the body inside medicine is different. I still had to put on shoes. I still had to stand at a dartboard. I still had to walk home. My toes still curled. My feet still hurt. Mechanical problems could still be real. Neurological problems could still be real. More than one mechanism could still coexist. Nothing about complexity required everything to belong to one explanation. But the record had made complexity directional. The Parkinsonism remained available for neurological development. It acquired DaT imaging, genetic hypotheses, specialist correspondence and treatment strategy. The foot problem acquired a vocabulary that increasingly made peripheral explanation its natural home.

That is how a description becomes an inheritance. A later clinician does not need to make the original separation again. It has already been made for them. They meet the words after the struggle that produced them has disappeared. They do not see Brennan holding feet, gait, tone and toe clawing together in one referral unless they go back and find it. They do not see the week in which the feet were barely investigated. They do not stand behind the curtain on the second Monday. They do not hear the bald-tires complaint. They do not watch “burning” enter the synthesis. They do not know how tentative the first neuropathy formulation was unless the qualification continues to travel with it.

They receive the descendant.

That is what makes wording consequential over time. Repetition can begin to look like corroboration even when the later language is descended from the earlier language. A phrase appears in one note, then another, then another. Each repetition makes it feel more historical, more settled, more independently observed. But sometimes the later sentence is not new evidence. It is the old classification speaking again through a new date. The feet became particularly vulnerable to that because the lived problem was messy. Corns were real. Callouses were real. Hammertoes were real. Pain was real. Toe curling was real. Abnormal movement was real. Treatments helped different pieces at different times. The messiness made partition feel plausible because each service could truthfully describe its own part. Podiatry could say what the toes looked like. Neurology could say what the Parkinsonism looked like. I could say what hurt. The record could keep all three statements and still lose the relationship among them.

That is the strange thing about records. They can preserve facts while changing the world those facts inhabit. By the time I began to understand this, I no longer saw the phrase “peripheral neuropathic pain symptomatologies” as merely technical language. I could see its ancestry. It had a grandfather. Perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy. And before that, an even earlier body: painful feet, toe clawing, abnormal gait, increased tone, trouble starting to move. The wording had become more confident in its object even where the underlying cause remained open. That is how the original uncertainty survived. It was no longer uncertainty about whether the feet had been correctly classified. It was uncertainty inside the classification. That distinction would matter enormously later, when the pain became severe enough to dominate my life and the record had to explain what was happening to me.

By then the vocabulary was waiting.

The feet did not need to be separated again. They had already been named into separation.

# Chapter Thirteen — Dog, Dog, Dog

By the time I bought the dog, I had every reason to think I was going upwards.

That is important, because otherwise the decision can look reckless in retrospect: a man with young-onset Parkinsonism, difficult feet and a body already carrying years of trouble goes out and buys a large black Labrador. But that is not how it looked from inside my life on 1 November 2021. I had spent the previous years getting better enough to trust improvement. In 2017, procyclidine had felt miraculous. I had written that my walk was back together, that I could stand straight, that my legs were loose and limber, that moving normally felt like living in a miracle. When the drug was stopped because of my eyesight, the deterioration came back quickly enough for Neurology to record it. In April 2018, the neurologist wrote that when I had been taken off procyclidine, “the first thing he noticed changing and changing rapidly” was the return of the outside-left-foot phenomenon, along with my becoming more shifty and unrelaxed. In the same clinic record he wrote: “Obviously he could be very much better if we would actually give him the levodopa or another dopamine agonist.” The reason for not doing so then was caution about the long-term consequences in somebody as young as me, not a belief that levodopa would be useless.

I did not read those words as a warning about the future. I experienced them as promise. I already knew what it was like for neurological treatment to transform my body. Now there was another treatment being held in reserve which, according to the doctor looking after me, could make me very much better again. That possibility sat quietly in the background for years while I got on with being alive. I worked. I played darts. I walked. I stopped drinking for a while. I travelled. In the year before I bought the dog I had been to Lanzarote four times. I was not going there to convalesce. I was swimming, going into town, eating ice cream, walking home after dinner, living days in which my body could recede into the scenery. I had become unintimidated by walking.

Then Sinemet arrived. The medical record confirms that I had been on forms of Sinemet from September 2021. By late October I was back in Neurology clinic. From where I stood, the sequence was encouraging rather than ominous: the first treatment had given me a version of my body I had almost forgotten was possible, years of ordinary movement had followed, and now the drug I had been told might make me better still had finally begun. I did not know what would happen next. I did not know which parts of my earlier treatment response would be remembered clinically and which would not. I certainly did not know that the changing relationship between neurological treatment and the pain in my feet would fail to travel forward with the same force as the categories already attached to them. I knew only that I had been well enough to travel repeatedly, that I could walk, and that I had just started the medication I had understood for years as the stronger card still waiting to be played.

So I bought a dog.

He was a black Labrador, a big intact male, eventually around forty-five kilos, and he did not arrive as a symbol. He arrived as work. Dogs do not care about diagnostic categories. They care that the door opens. They care about food, smells, routine, whatever is happening on the other side of a hedge, and the non-negotiable fact that at some point somebody has to put a lead on them and go outside. At the beginning I walked him four times a day, roughly nine in the morning, one in the afternoon, six in the evening and eleven at night. That was the structure. The dog needed walking, so I walked him. I lost a substantial amount of weight. We became great pals.

There was pleasure in the obligation because I had bought him at the crest of something. He belonged to the version of my future in which movement was available. A Labrador is not a cautious purchase if walking frightens you. Mine was bought because walking did not frighten me. I had spent years learning again that I could trust my legs over distance. I could leave the house without measuring every pavement against the possibility of not getting home. The dog fitted naturally into that life. Four walks a day did not sound like a sentence. They sounded like a routine.

He also fitted into the rest of me. I sometimes brought him to Taylor’s. Once I had him, I found myself wanting a pint at night again and he could come with me. That is how life actually works: habits return through other habits. You are not living inside a treatment plan. You are living inside evenings, pubs, weather, cigarettes, dogs, friends, and whatever bargain you are making with yourself that week. I could have a pint and bring the dog. I could walk home. The animal expanded the day because he required the day to keep happening.

The change was gradual enough that there was no single morning on which I woke and thought: this is now a different life. That would have been easier. Instead, distance began acquiring weight again. Pace began mattering. The dog, who had no interest in my explanations, became one of the first clear measures of what my body could still supply. He would move at the speed of a healthy Labrador and I would move at whatever speed was available to me. Sometimes he watched my foot when it went still or quivered. I do not need to give that behaviour a human meaning. He noticed movement because dogs notice movement. What mattered was that the thing at the end of the lead was becoming less reliable.

There is something humiliating about failing an animal in a way the animal does not accuse you of. A person can understand that you are ill. A dog only knows the walk is slower, shorter, interrupted, or late. There were days when I walked behind him crying and apologised for being such a disabled owner. I felt I was not giving him the pace he deserved. That sounds almost ridiculous written down. He was fed. He was loved. He was walked. But guilt does not measure itself against a veterinary checklist. It measures itself against the version of you who bought the dog. I had bought him because I believed I could give him a big life. As mine contracted, I felt his life contracting beside it.

The last walk of the night became the best hiding place for what was happening to me. Eleven o’clock, roughly. Everybody else’s day was closing. Mine still had one obligation left. Whatever had happened before that — pain, tiredness, stiffness, frustration, drink, medication, the accumulated cost of the previous three walks — the dog still needed out. The degree of struggle on those walks went unseen night after night after night, hundreds of times. There was no audience for it and therefore no performance to maintain. No clinic. No letter. No one asking how far I could walk. Just a man and a dog moving through the end of the day, one of us carrying the lead and the other one setting a pace.

This is where the dog became something I had not intended him to become. At first I walked him because I could. Later I walked him because he needed me to. The distinction matters. When your own reasons for doing something become negotiable, responsibility to another creature is harder to bargain with. I could decide that I was too tired. I could decide pain had won. I could decide there was no point going out again. The dog could not decide any of those things for me. He still needed the door opened.

I was asked later about the days when he forced me out of the chair. I realised the question was larger than a chair. At the heart of it was: when do you give up? With a dog, you do not only have to decide when you are prepared to give up on yourself. You would have to give up on him first. That was the line I came to understand: “As soon as you give up on the dog, you’ll give up on yourself.” It was not inspirational. It was logistical. He needed walking. Therefore I had to stand up.

Years into the relationship, when the medical story had become darker than anything I could have imagined on the day I bought him, I read an obituary on RIP.ie for a young man who had worked in a pub and had died by suicide. Among the people and family named as being left behind, the obituary mentioned his dog. That undid me. I looked at my own dog and wept. By then I knew perfectly well how much physical effort he had cost me. I also knew what that effort had given back. The thought came almost complete: “You nearly killed me, but you keep me alive.”

Both halves were true. He could exhaust me. He could force me into one more walk when one more walk felt absurd. He could make me cry from the gap between what I wanted to give him and what my body could provide. But he also made surrender complicated. There was always another living thing waiting for me to get up. I had bought him when I believed the future was getting larger. He stayed while it got smaller.

And still, at the end of the day, there was the lead.

# Chapter Fourteen — Lyrica Removed the Foot Pain

By 2023 my feet were no longer the feet that had entered hospital in 2017. They hurt in the same places, but I was no longer medication-naive. That distinction sounds technical until you understand what it means. In 2017 I had arrived with years of trouble in my feet, abnormal gait, toe clawing, tension and pain. There was no burning then. There had been no burning before it, and there would be no burning until after Sinemet was introduced in 2021. The word appeared in the neurological synthesis anyway, beside the suggestion of peripheral neuropathy. Later, after treatment had changed my body, burning became real to me. I began using the word myself. Read without chronology, that looks like confirmation. Read with chronology, it is something much more disturbing. The record acquired the symptom before I did.

The first medications had changed my body dramatically. Procyclidine gave me a release from tension that felt miraculous. When it was stopped because of my eyesight, my feet became restless and my walk began falling apart. In April 2018 the neurologist recorded a cleaner version of the same fact. Off procyclidine, “the first thing he noticed changing and changing rapidly was the return on the foot, of the outside left corn.” I became more “shifty” and “unrelaxed.” He recorded the response. He knew that what happened in my feet could change quickly when neurological treatment changed.

That did not prove what every painful thing in my feet was. I had real corns. I had callouses. I had structural problems. Podiatry treated them. What it proved was more basic: the feet were pharmacologically modifiable. Medication was not background. It belonged inside the case.

By 2020 I was travelling and walking for pleasure. I had stopped being intimidated by walking. By late 2021 I was on Sinemet. By 2022 Neurology recorded that I adjusted Sinemet according to expected demands such as playing darts. It also recorded that even relatively small amounts of levodopa could produce excessive movement, and that I remembered being “very obviously dyskinetic” on the higher Stalevo dose. The neurologist described the balancing problem openly. Too little dopaminergic effect and I could become stiff and akinetic. Too much and I could become choreiform and dyskinetic. The drug was not simply helping me. It was changing the state of my body.

What happened inside my shoes was harder to see. Before levodopa my feet could feel soft, sensitive, almost concave, as though I were collapsing into them. Later they could become hard, hurting, convex things, pushed into a different shape and load. I did not have a clinical language for that. I had the feet. I knew that the old territory had become more painful. I knew that movement itself now changed according to medication state. I knew that the same treatment which could release movement could also produce excessive movement. By then the clinically relevant object was no longer simply Parkinson's disease plus an unrelated painful foot. It was Parkinson's disease under treatment, with a foot phenotype being changed by treatment.

The old record had a container ready for the pain.

Peripheral neuropathy.

Nobody had established one in 2017. The discharge synthesis had said the leg symptoms, including corns, callouses and burning, “may have been completely separate or perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy,” perhaps related in some respect to alcohol. “Not clear.” The uncertainty was explicit. But uncertainty can become strangely durable once it has been given a noun. Later in the same record PARK9 appeared as a possible genetic explanation for “the neuropathy question.” A question had become a thing. It could be referred to, carried forward and worked on.

There is another record which makes the contrast almost comic. In 2019, when my GP referred me back to podiatry for clawing feet, pain, increased tone and abnormal gait relating to Parkinson's disease, the podiatry form marked me low risk and ticked “No neuropathy.” That did not rule out every neuropathy I could ever develop. It does something more interesting. It shows that while Neurology was carrying “the neuropathy question,” another clinical record could look at the same patient and write the opposite category in plain language: no neuropathy.

By 2023 the burning pain was severe. This was not the burning of the 2017 synthesis. This was something I could feel. By then the feet were carrying almost every kind of trouble at once: dystonia, dyskinesia, corns, callouses, ingrown nails, cramping, focal pressure pain and burning. Walking could hurt at more than one point inside a single step — loading the foot, releasing it, transferring onto the other side. There was barely a neutral phase left in the act of walking. My GP described severe bilateral “burning” in both feet and lower legs, at times extreme, causing what he called a death wish. Pregabalin had been pushed from 25 milligrams twice daily through escalating doses to 200 milligrams three times daily: 600 milligrams a day. My GP wrote that this was only just controlling the symptoms and that he was worried about me. The Parkinson's nurse recorded ongoing debilitating pain and no quality of life. The man who had once travelled to walk was now organising his day around surviving his feet.

There was another fact in the GP's urgent referral which should have mattered enormously. He reminded Neurology that I had done something similar years earlier with gabapentin for the shoulder and arm, and that those pains had then “suddenly” settled with better control of the Parkinson's. In other words, the referral itself contained a warning from my own history: severe pain in me had previously changed when the neurological treatment changed.

By now, attributing the burning pain to a stable separate neuropathic process required ignoring the pharmacological system in which the pain was occurring. I was no longer the untreated man of 2017. Sinemet was in my bloodstream and acting in my brain. Its effects were visible in my movement. Too little dopaminergic effect could leave me slow, tight and dystonic. Too much could make me dyskinetic. My feet were moving inside that oscillation. The pain existed inside it too.

That is the point at which I now regard the pain as iatrogenic. I do not mean that levodopa was a poison and that every molecule of Sinemet produced burning on contact. I mean something more clinically serious. The treatment helped create the state in which the pain occurred. Dose, timing and formulation were governing tone, movement, posture, loading and the transition between dystonia and dyskinesia. The patient was being moved pharmacologically between two painful mechanical possibilities. The same drug could improve one part of the foot state and worsen another. The question was no longer simply what disease was in my feet. It was what disease plus treatment had made of them.

The 2023 neurology note comes remarkably close to saying this without putting the pieces together. It records cramping in the left lower limb and explicitly raises “the question of a Parkinsonian dystonia whether an ON or OFF dystonia,” adding that an OFF dystonia was clearly more likely. It records that I took Stalevo in the evening to facilitate darts. It says the evenings sounded like “typical OFF Parkinson's evening symptoms.” It records choreiform movement. It notes that extra levodopa on darts nights could produce extra movement. It says that improvement in pain when I took Stalevo could be understood as part of a Parkinsonian non-motor fluctuation.

Then comes one of the most important sentences in the note: “It is not in the nature of a ‘peripheral neuropathy’ to come on literally overnight.”

There it is. The neurologist himself produces counter-evidence against peripheral neuropathy as the explanation for the new acute change.

And the neuropathy survives.

A few lines later he describes “the previous ‘peripheral neuropathy question’” as having “seemed to subside.” That is the second appearance of those two words travelling together across the record: neuropathy question. The first had been PARK9, “the neuropathy question.” Six years later it has become “the previous peripheral neuropathy question.” The uncertainty has acquired a biography.

Then, just when that old hypothesis should have been in danger of collapsing under the weight of what had accumulated against it, another investigation appeared. Fabry disease.

The neurologist sent alpha-galactosidase levels because Fabry disease can overlap Parkinsonism and peripheral neuropathy. Later he described the test as an attempt to ensure that I did not have coexistent Fabry disease which might have caused my Parkinson's disease and “indeed neuropathic symptoms.”

Which symptoms?

The record does not answer that question by pointing to a demonstrated neuropathy. It does not identify a nerve-conduction study establishing it. It does not identify a clean sensory syndrome which had converted the 2017 “perhaps” into a finding. It points back to the feet and the pain already living in the neuropathy branch. The grammar had moved further than the evidence. “Perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy” became “the neuropathy question,” then “the previous peripheral neuropathy question,” then “neuropathic symptoms.” The uncertainty had not disappeared. It had changed address.

This is how a hypothesis takes on a life of its own. When counter-evidence arrives, the hypothesis does not necessarily die. It can generate another investigation downstream. Fabry did not reopen the question of whether I had a neuropathy. It assumed neuropathy strongly enough to go looking for a rarer cause for it.

The treatment consequences were no longer abstract. In the same consultation the neurologist wrote that he had been planning Tegretol, amitriptyline or an SSRI/SNRI, drugs used for neuropathy, “in case this was a peripheral neuropathic disorder.” Lyrica was to remain at 600 milligrams. Increasing evening levodopa was also considered, but there was the known risk of chorea. The clinician could therefore balance one drug against another: Lyrica for the supposed neuropathic pain, Sinemet for the Parkinsonism, with levodopa constrained by dyskinesia.

The problem is obvious once the original classification is put back on trial. They were balancing Lyrica against Sinemet before they had securely established what Lyrica was treating.

This was not merely a semantic mistake. The uncertain category was determining pharmacology.

The 2024 note makes that visible in one sentence: “The Lyrica removed the foot pain – the right foot is much better, there is still some in the left. But it isn’t relieving the toe curling in when playing darts.” “Removed” is followed immediately by pain that remains. The pain and the toe curling are partitioned again. Lyrica appears to succeed against one object while a movement phenomenon survives beside it. A few lines later the neurologist again describes the “classic balancing problem”: help me in the evenings with dopamine, but do not push me so far that I become dyskinetic.

I do not need to pretend Lyrica did nothing. It helped at times. That is not the same thing as proving neuropathy. Treatment response is evidence, not diagnosis. My neurologist had demonstrated that principle beautifully with my shoulder years earlier. Gabapentin could change pain without proving mechanism. Steroids could change pain without proving mechanism. With the feet, however, benefit from a drug commonly used for neuropathic pain could sit comfortably inside the neuropathy story, while failure at the maximum dose did not force the story to reopen.

From 2022 through 2025 I moved up and down on Lyrica. I reached the maximum 600 milligrams a day. Later I began reducing it myself. By 2025 Neurology recorded the reduction from 600 milligrams to 50 milligrams three times daily and specifically recorded that I had reduced it myself. The record then used the fact that my pain deteriorated after the reduction as part of the case for Lyrica. That fact is real. But by itself it cannot tell us what caused the pain. It tells us only that pregabalin affected it.

The 2025 record is where the explanatory architecture becomes clearest. The neurologist wrote: “He knows very well that once the Sinemet hits his system that the feet feel better from a pain perspective.” He had “little doubt” that more Sinemet would give further relief. But more Sinemet would also produce more severe dyskinesia. His solution was to consider increasing Lyrica instead, specifically to avoid increasing the Parkinson's medication.

Later, in the formal complaint response, he made that treatment logic explicit. Lyrica, he wrote, had been introduced for “the neuropathic pain, of whatever cause, in part to try to spare further Sinemet increase.” The phrase is astonishingly revealing. “Neuropathic pain” is certain enough to govern treatment. Its cause can remain “whatever.” Lyrica is then used as a pharmacological counterweight so that Sinemet does not have to be increased and dyskinesia does not worsen.

By then the record had created a closed explanatory system. If Lyrica helped, that fitted neuropathic treatment. If maximum-dose Lyrica only partly helped, the answer could be more pain management. If I reduced Lyrica and the pain worsened, that supported Lyrica's role. If Sinemet helped the feet, that did not have to challenge the neuropathy category because the neurologist could say Parkinson's merely magnified the experience of pain from some other cause. If controlled-release Sinemet later produced profound relief, that too could be described as a beneficial effect on the experience of pain rather than evidence that the old partition needed to be rebuilt.

That is what the complaint response does. It says Parkinson's can magnify pain “from other causes.” It says pain can worsen when Sinemet wears off. It accepts that when Sinemet is working there is less experience of pain. It describes this as a known non-motor effect. The drug is therefore allowed to modify the pain without being allowed to identify the pain.

Sinemet is cast as beneficial to the patient's experience. What disappears is the other half of the pharmacological equation: Sinemet also changes the movement state which produces the feet themselves. It can release dystonia. It can produce dyskinesia. It can change the walk, the loading, the posture, the amount of movement and the shape of the foot under load. Once that is true, saying that Sinemet “helps the experience of pain” is not enough. The clinically necessary question is what the dosing pattern is doing to the painful body across the whole cycle.

That question becomes unavoidable in 2025. On 10 March I was prescribed Sinemet controlled release. I later described the effect as categorical: the foot pain fell, walking improved, and the suicidal quality of the suffering receded. In May, when I was trying to obtain help for dystonia, I described myself as positioned between two well-known risks: “untreated dystonia and medication-induced dyskinesia.” That was the physical problem stripped of the record's inherited categories. Too little effective dopaminergic treatment and dystonia could dominate. Too much and medication-induced dyskinesia could dominate. I was trying to live in the narrow space between them.

By July I was splitting controlled-release and immediate-release doses and managing the narrow therapeutic window myself. By August I wrote to my GP that Sinemet CR given overnight had demonstrated that the pain was dopaminergic-responsive. My GP replied that he could not adjust levodopa without specialist guidance. The pharmacology had become so central that primary care would not touch it independently.

This is why the burning pain of 2023 cannot be read innocently as the late confirmation of a neuropathy suggested in 2017. The burning entered my lived experience after the pharmacological treatment had transformed the movement disorder. By 2023 I was in severe psychological distress inside a body whose tone and movement were being actively manipulated by dopaminergic medication. The pain was occurring in the same feet, but the causal system around those feet had changed.

The failure of maximum-dose Lyrica did not mathematically prove that no neuropathic mechanism existed. It did something more important in this particular history. It removed any justification for allowing neuropathy to remain the privileged explanation without reopening the whole case. The record already contained the alternative mechanism: medication-state dependence, dystonia, OFF symptoms, dyskinesia, levodopa responsiveness. The safeguarding failure was not that nobody possessed enough clues. It was that there was no mechanism forcing those clues to defeat the old category.

The same neurologist was upstream from all of it. He had written the early synthesis. He knew how provisional “perhaps” had been. He knew what “Not clear” meant because it was his uncertainty. He knew that “the neuropathy question” had begun as a question. Everybody else could inherit the noun. He inherited his own provenance.

That matters when the later response says, “Whatever the underlying cause for the peripheral neuropathic pain symptomatologies that Liam has...” By then the uncertainty has moved again. It is no longer whether the pain is neuropathic. The pain is now grammatically possessed: neuropathic pain symptomatologies that I have. Only the “underlying cause” remains open.

The original question has disappeared inside the sentence.

I did not know any of this in 2023. I knew that my feet burned. I knew the pain frightened me. I knew the dog still had to be walked. I knew I was taking more medication and getting less life. I knew that some doses made movement easier and some states made my body feel like a different mechanical object. I did not know that a hypothesis written six years earlier was still deciding what kind of pain I was allowed to have.

By 2025 the body had forced a different proposition onto the record. Once Sinemet hit my system, the feet felt better. More Sinemet would probably help further, but at the price of more dyskinesia. Controlled-release Sinemet changed the result again. The patient was not living with neuropathy on one side and Parkinson's treatment on the other. He was living inside a treatment-dependent movement disorder in which the feet changed as the treatment changed.

The neuropathy question had survived almost everything.

Then the pharmacology answered back.

# Chapter Fifteen — Profound Relief

On 10 March 2025, I walked out of Neurology with a follow-up appointment for one year later.

I knew I would not be at it.

I thought I would be dead.

That is not hindsight and it is not a literary improvement on what I meant. I had already used the language of a death wish. I had told the neurologist that saying “I won't live with it” was how I put words on the situation. I was not joking. The first hundred and fifty steps of the morning could feel like torture. The feet could be too sore to tie my runners. Pain was no longer one difficulty inside a life. It had begun to determine whether I could imagine continuing the life at all.

I left the clinic carrying a Galway University Hospitals prescription sheet. Tom had written it while I sat in front of him. He had often described the amount of Sinemet I was taking as a paediatric dose. While he was writing, I asked him whether I was still on the paediatric dose.

“Yes.”

I remember him seeming anxious at the beginning of the consultation. I cannot know what was in his mind. I can only preserve the impression I had while sitting opposite him. By the time I left, I remember no eye contact between us.

The prescription contained Half Sinemet CR, 25/100. I had also asked whether I could see a private pain specialist because the pain was not going away. I do not remember Tom explaining to me in the room the reasoning that later appeared in his dictated letter about amitriptyline or more Lyrica. What I understood when I left was simpler. I was not being referred. There was no urgent review. There was no safety plan. There was no discussion about what I should do if the statement “I won't live with it” became an action rather than a description. The follow-up was twelve months.

I did not rush to the pharmacy.

That delay matters. If I had walked straight from the clinic to collect the controlled-release Sinemet, the chronology would look cleaner than the life was. For three days I sat with the existential fact of what had happened in that room. I was not waiting excitedly to try a new tablet. I was asking myself whether this was where the story ended.

On Thursday, 13 March, I collected the box. I spent the rest of the day deliberately resting. At eleven o'clock that night, I swallowed the first tablet.

The following morning I got out of bed.

For years the first steps had been a negotiation. By March 2025 they had become something worse. Every phase of walking could hurt: the foot taking weight, the body transferring, the foot releasing, the other foot accepting the next step. Pain had colonised the mechanics of walking.

On 14 March I put my foot on the floor and there was no immediate agony.

No bolts. No contorted first steps. No waiting for the body to become inhabitable. No psychological bracing before movement. I stood up and walked.

I later tried to describe that morning and found myself reaching for language that sounded almost religious: a day I had never had, a privilege to experience for the first time in twenty years, stepping onto the floor without ten thousand bolts of pain shooting through me to welcome me into the day. The exaggeration, if there is one, is in the number. The change itself did not feel exaggerated. It felt categorical.

What should have been astonishing relief became grief almost immediately.

I knew at once that I had been living inside a distorted reality. I did not know the nature of the distortion. I did not know its scale or how far backwards it reached. I did not yet know what had happened in 2017, how the language had moved through the record, or what parts of the old explanation would survive being examined. But I knew something simpler and more frightening: the story that had been passing between me and my neurologist was not reality.

The pain had not merely eased. The morning had changed species.

That is the moment around which the rest of this story turns.

The clinic letter from 10 March preserved the contradiction in unusually vivid terms. The one thing on my mind was foot pain. The first hundred and fifty footsteps were torture. Yet the organising explanation in the same note still allowed for “a degree of peripheral neuropathy.” The old hypothesis was still alive. Alcohol was gone, which was described as potentially protective if neuropathy was present. Lyrica had already been taken to its maximum. I had reduced it myself. The feet remained the problem.

There was also another fact in the same record. Once the Parkinson's medication hit my system, the feelings in the feet improved. The neurologist wrote that I knew "very well" that once Sinemet hit, the feet felt better from a pain perspective. He thought more Sinemet would probably produce more relief. The difficulty was dyskinesia. Push the dopamine further and the body might move too much.

That was the balancing problem as it had come to be described: pain on one side, dyskinesia on the other, Lyrica somewhere in the middle helping to spare further increases in Sinemet. The treatment plan had become an exercise in managing the consequences of a conceptual division made years earlier. Neuropathic pain belonged to one branch. Parkinson's belonged to another. Lyrica could be increased for the first. Sinemet could be constrained for the second.

Then one night the controlled-release tablet crossed the border.

I did not wake with a theory. I woke without the pain.

That distinction matters. The interpretation came afterwards. The body moved first.

I had spent years accepting that treatment could improve one part of me without explaining another. That had been the logic of the record from the beginning. Procyclidine could make me looser. Rasagiline could help. Dopaminergic treatment could alter the gait. Lyrica could help the pain. Corns could be corns. Toe curling could be toe curling. Neuropathy could remain a question. Parkinson's could remain the diagnosis. Each thing could be improved locally without requiring the whole arrangement to be reconsidered.

The morning of 14 March made that arrangement much harder to sustain.

If a change in Parkinson's medication could transform the feet overnight, then the feet could no longer be treated as though dopaminergic treatment merely stood outside the problem and changed how much I happened to notice it. The treatment was inside the phenomenon. It was changing what the feet did.

I did not yet understand exactly how.

That uncertainty is important. I did not wake up knowing the final mechanism. I did not know how much of the old pain was dystonia, how much was the movement created by treatment, how much was injury from years of abnormal loading, how much was inflammation, or how much belonged to other causes. I did not know whether controlled release was helping because it covered the overnight OFF period, because it smoothed the rise and fall of levodopa, because it changed tone, or because several of those things were true at once.

What I knew was simpler.

The feet had changed because the treatment had changed.

That fact reached backward.

It reached back to 2017, when I had been medication naive and the feet had entered hospital inside the same body as the abnormal gait, increased tone and dystonic-looking lower limb. It reached back to the first months of treatment, when procyclidine had changed the tension and walking so dramatically that I described myself as a melted snowman, and when stopping it brought the foot problem rapidly back. It reached back to 2018, when the neurologist recorded that withdrawal of procyclidine had produced the rapid return of the outside-left-foot problem. It reached forward again through the years of levodopa sensitivity, dyskinesia, OFF symptoms, toe curling and a body increasingly dependent on medication state.

I had spent years being taught to understand these as separate facts.

Now they began to behave like a sequence.

Pain is greedy. Severe pain occupies attention before you have decided to give it anything. It shapes mornings, shoes, routes, conversations, mood, sleep, the distance to the door, the obligation of the dog, the likelihood of standing up once you have sat down. It fills the mental room. When it suddenly retreats, the room does not stay empty.

For me, meaning entered immediately.

I wrote later that the body had quieted and the meaning had erupted. That is as close as I can get to it. The relief and the grief were not sequential events separated by reflection. They arrived together. The body had produced a fact so large that the history behind it became unstable at once.

If this could happen now, what had the previous eight years been?

I began trying to get somebody to help me answer that.

On 10 March, after the neurology appointment, I contacted my GP practice and asked for a phone consultation. I wanted to send a written brief beforehand because I doubted my ability to explain the issue clearly enough on a call. That detail matters to me now. I was already trying to protect the problem from being compressed by conversation. I wanted the clinician to have the whole thing in front of him before we spoke.

My GP was away. I was offered another doctor or the option of waiting. The choice came back to me.

The next day I started making calls. I tried the old Parkinson's nurse line even though it had been retired. I called a neurologist familiar with movement disorders. I called my acupuncturist because, whatever else I thought, I still needed relief. The chronology is almost embarrassing in its simplicity. I had a problem. The problem had become dangerous to live with. I began assembling a care pathway myself.

Then the tablet worked.

That should have made the next step easier. Instead it made the problem larger.

A week earlier I had been asking how to survive the pain. Now I was asking what the relief meant.

On 19 March I filed a formal complaint. I described diagnostic anchoring, failure to reassess and the contradiction created by the Sinemet CR response. I asked the most obvious question available to me at the time: why had this possibility not been explored earlier, given the magnitude of what I had endured?

I did not have the full record yet. I did not know then what I know now about the movement of words through the chart, about the phrase "the neuropathy question," about the way "perhaps" became pathway, about the way a hypothesis can survive by changing what remains uncertain. I knew only that something important had happened in my body and that the existing explanation could no longer contain it.

That was enough to change my relationship with the case.

Before March 2025, I had mostly lived inside medicine's explanation of me. Even when I disagreed with parts of it, I still assumed the larger account existed somewhere above my own understanding. Doctors knew more than I did. The file knew more than I did. Somewhere there was a coherent history that made sense of the choices that had been made.

After 14 March, I began to suspect that the coherence itself might be the problem.

Relief did that.

Not anger. Not litigation. Not a theory about diagnostic bias. Relief.

My body gave me a result that the story could not comfortably absorb.

In the weeks that followed, the result became more complicated rather than less. Controlled-release Sinemet was not a magic door through which I walked permanently into a pain-free life. The therapeutic window was narrow. Too little effective dopaminergic treatment and the foot could tighten, collapse inward and hurt. Too much and I could overshoot into dyskinesia, restlessness and a different kind of mechanical trouble. I began splitting doses, comparing controlled-release and immediate-release formulations, noticing how long each took to act, how long the benefit lasted, how the gait changed, what happened after food, what happened after walking, what happened when the dose was too much.

By May I described myself as positioned between two medically recognisable risks: untreated dystonia and medication-induced dyskinesia.

That sentence is more important to me than any later theory. It describes the world I was actually living in. I was no longer asking whether Sinemet was good or bad. The drug could relieve one pathological state and create another. The problem was not merely dose. It was state.

Too little and I could be trapped in painful dystonia. Too much and I could be thrown into excessive movement. Somewhere between them was a quieter body.

By June I was mapping that window almost obsessively. Half tablets. Immediate release. Controlled release. Time to onset. Pain scores. Tone. Stride length. Restlessness. Shoes. Walking. I did not solve my body. I modelled it. Pain fell within minutes of small doses on some occasions. Full daytime controlled release could push me too far. At other times controlled release at night seemed to bridge the morning more gently. Dystonia and dyskinesia could alternate. Sometimes they seemed to coexist. The feet were not a static peripheral object. They were moving through pharmacological states.

This later work did not retroactively make every conclusion I drew at the time correct. I was a patient experimenting because I was trying to stay functional, not a laboratory running a controlled trial. Some of the hypotheses I entertained would change. Some would be wrong. But the observations themselves mattered. Dose, timing, movement and pain were connected. That connection was reproducible enough that I could begin navigating by it.

And once I saw that, the old formulation became almost impossible to inhabit.

For years the question had been what caused the neuropathic pain.

The better question was what was happening to the feet across the treatment cycle.

The distinction sounds small. It is not.

One question begins with a category and searches for its cause. The other begins with the body and asks what changes it.

I had spent most of my adult life answering the first question without knowing I had accepted its premise.

Now, for the first time, I was watching the second question generate answers.

The relief itself did not prove everything. It did something more consequential. It made the old certainty impossible.

On 14 March 2025, I stepped out of bed and the first hundred and fifty steps were no longer torture.

The pain had gone quiet enough for me to hear the record.

Relief did not close the case.

It reopened 2017.

# Chapter Sixteen — Tom and Liam

The last thing Thomas Monaghan wrote for me while I was sitting in front of him was not an explanation of the pain.

It was a warning about constipation.

By then we had known each other for nearly eight years. I had been admitted to hospital as a frightened thirty-nine-year-old who could barely move properly and came out with a diagnosis that explained enough of what was happening to make the future feel possible again. Tom had been there at the beginning of that. He had looked at me and said, “You’ve got Parkinsonism.” During those first hospital days he came down to see me at the end of his day, making me his last patient on several evenings. I liked the conversations and I believe he did too. I listened hard. I asked questions that went beyond the obvious ones. On Tuesday 4 July, after the Complex Case Meeting, he came down looking, to me, forlorn and told me that his boss had said not to give me the good stuff. The good stuff was levodopa. I remember that openness because it felt personal rather than procedural. He had seemed calm and reasonable. I did not leave hospital thinking I had met an enemy. I left thinking I had met a doctor who had found something important.

When people ask why I trusted him for so long, the question sounds stranger to me than the answer.

You just trust a doctor. You do not begin by looking for reasons to trust or distrust.

Calling him Tom was never a declaration of friendship. Years behind a bar had made first names ordinary to me; if I knew your name, I used it. In the clinic we often sat too close and talked too directly for titles to do much work anyway. Over time “Tom” simply became the neutral name in my head.

That was especially true when treatment worked. Procyclidine released the tension from my body. Walking came back. I felt like a melted snowman. I returned to work. I travelled. I played darts. I could walk powerfully enough that for a time I was unintimidated by walking. Whatever questions remained around the feet did not dominate my understanding because, in practical terms, I was better. The neurologist had not explicitly solved the feet, but the treatment had made them easier to live with. At the time, that was good enough for me.

Trust is not an agreement to inspect every sentence written about you.

It is almost the opposite.

It is the decision, usually unconscious, that somebody else is keeping track.

Over the years the relationship became familiar. Letters accumulated. Appointments accumulated. Treatment changed. We talked about work, alcohol, darts, travel, medication, pain, mood, movement, side effects. There were moments of kindness and moments of good medicine. There were moments when he was intellectually curious and moments when he was careful. The shoulder episode later showed me exactly what that could look like. When he did not know, he could say so. He could localise a problem, build a differential, investigate anatomy, reject an explanation that did not fit and write, “I genuinely do not know what the cause is here.”

That matters to me.

If I make him stupid, the story becomes easier and less true.

He was not stupid.

If I make him uncaring, the story becomes easier and less true.

I do not think he was uncaring.

The problem is more serious than either of those things.

A doctor can care about a patient and still preserve the wrong architecture around him. A relationship can be warm and still contain a profound inequality of information. A patient can know his body intimately while remaining ignorant of how the official story of that body was constructed. A doctor can know both the patient and the provenance of the record.

That was the inequality I did not understand while I was living inside it.

I knew what my feet felt like.

He knew what had been written about them.

More importantly, he knew how it had come to be written.

I did not.

For years I met each new sentence as though it had arrived from medicine itself. “Peripheral neuropathy.” “The neuropathy question.” “Neuropathic symptoms.” “Peripheral neuropathic pain symptomatologies.” I had no reason to distinguish between a finding and an inheritance. If a later clinician used the language, I assumed the language had earned its place somehow. That is one of the ordinary meanings of trust. You assume there is a chain behind the conclusion.

I did not know that I had seen the chain begin.

I had been there when the feet were not dealt with.

I had been there when I forced them back into the room.

I had been there when the response was not a focused neurological reinvestigation of the feet but an explanation which allowed them to sit outside the newly recognised Parkinsonian syndrome.

What I did not know was that this explanation would become durable enough for later clinicians, including me, to inherit it as history.

By March 2025 the relationship between Tom and me had become almost unbearably concentrated around the feet.

The consultation on 10 March is extraordinary to read now because almost every element of the problem is present in the note.

“The one thing” on my mind was foot pain.

Since 2005, I said, my feet had disturbed me. I hobbled out of bed. They could be too sore to tie my runners. The first 150 footsteps were torture. I called it “murder in the feet.” I said that putting the words “I won’t live with it” on the situation was the only way I could adequately describe the severity.

The same note records that once the Parkinson’s medication hit my system, the feelings in my feet improved.

Tom wrote that I knew “very well” that once Sinemet hit my system my feet felt better from a pain perspective. He believed more Sinemet would probably produce further relief. But more Sinemet also threatened more severe dyskinesia.

The therapeutic trap was therefore visible in the note itself.

Pain.

Dopaminergic responsiveness.

Dyskinesia.

Maximum-dose Lyrica which had not eliminated the pain.

A patient describing a life becoming unlivable.

And then I asked whether I could see a private chronic pain specialist. The reason was hardly obscure. The pain was not going away.

I do not remember Tom giving me, in the room, the explanation that later appears in the letter. His clinic letters were often dictated as the consultation unfolded, into speech-recognition software, sometimes with typing as well. Sitting opposite him, I could not know which words were being captured and which were not. What the finished note says is that he suspected a pain specialist would probably recommend amitriptyline or increase the Lyrica. It also records my own point that at 600 milligrams of Lyrica the pain had certainly not been eliminated.

Then the request disappears.

There is no pain-specialist referral recorded there. No alternative service. No urgent review. No written explanation of why specialist pain assessment would add nothing. No new investigation generated by the fact that maximal pregabalin had failed to eliminate the thing being treated as neuropathic pain.

Follow-up: one year.

That was almost the end of the letter.

But I left the room with another document.

A Galway University Hospitals prescription sheet.

Prescription pads are ordinarily instruments for giving something. This one also contained a prohibition.

In his handwriting, Tom wrote that he had no objection to Nurofen or ibuprofen one to three times a week, but not more, and not Nurofen Plus because of constipation. While he was writing the sheet I asked him whether I was still on what he had long called a paediatric dose of Sinemet. He said yes.

I remember no eye contact as I left.

There was a safeguard on the page.

That is what makes the page so hard for me to look at now.

The safeguarding was real.

It was pointed at constipation.

I do not write that because constipation is trivial. Codeine causes it. In Parkinson’s disease, bowel problems matter. Caution around an opioid can be clinically sensible. The point is not that he should have ignored that risk.

The point is what the document proves about attention.

A foreseeable medication consequence could be identified, written down and converted into a clear behavioural instruction before I left the room.

Do not take this.

Do not take too much of that.

Here is the risk.

Meanwhile the larger danger already visible in the same encounter had no comparable written architecture around it. I had described the first steps of every morning as torture. The pain was severe enough for me to describe life itself as conditional. My feet improved when Sinemet came on. More Sinemet threatened dyskinesia. Lyrica at its maximum dose had not eliminated the pain. I asked about a pain specialist.

I remember no safeguarding conversation. No question about whether I was at immediate risk from myself. No crisis plan. No instruction about who to call if I could no longer keep myself safe. No urgent review. No attempt to bring the follow-up closer than the date written in the letter.

One year.

The last risk he protected me from was constipation.

And yet the same prescription sheet contained something else.

Half Sinemet CR.

There it was, on the page beside the prohibition. A controlled-release version of the drug which the clinic note already acknowledged changed the feelings in my feet. It was a modest treatment adjustment. No one wrote it as a diagnostic challenge. No one presented it to me as a test capable of reopening eight years of medical history.

It was simply the whiff of relief.

Three nights later I took the first tablet at eleven o’clock. By the next morning the pain had changed categorically, and with it my understanding of the relationship.

That is where Tom and Liam separated for me.

Not into hero and villain. Not into friend and enemy.

Into two people who had occupied the same clinical relationship without possessing the same history of it.

I had the body.

He had the provenance.

Until that morning, I had trusted that those two things had been joined somewhere in the record.

They had not.

# Chapter Seventeen — As the Letters Show

The first complaint I made after the Sinemet CR response was not complicated.

I wanted to know how this had happened.

That question is different from asking whether the treatment I had received could be defended.

I was not asking for a polished account of why each individual decision might have seemed reasonable when viewed in isolation. I was asking how the feet that entered hospital with the rest of me in 2017 had come to spend eight years living in a separate explanatory system.

How did “perhaps” become history?

How did “Not clear” become treatment?

How did an unproven peripheral neuropathy become something sufficiently real to organise drugs, referrals and later interpretation while remaining insufficiently real to demand a decisive neurological investigation of its own?

Those were genealogical questions.

The answer I eventually received was historical in a different sense.

The record defended itself by pointing back to the record.

“As the letters show.”

The phrase is almost perfect.

By then there were many letters. That was precisely the problem. A proposition repeated across eight years does not necessarily represent eight independent acts of discovery. Sometimes it represents one early proposition travelling successfully.

The response to my complaint set out a coherent account of the treatment history. It explained that Sinemet could improve the experience of pain “of whatever cause.” It said Parkinson’s disease can magnify pain that originates elsewhere. It pointed to dyskinesia as a reason not to push levodopa indefinitely. It noted that Lyrica had been introduced for the neuropathic pain, “of whatever cause,” in part to spare further increases in Sinemet. It noted that I had reduced Lyrica myself and that the pain later deteriorated. It acknowledged that controlled-release Sinemet had recently provided profound relief.

Read quickly, it sounds like coherence.

Read genealogically, it reveals something else.

“Whatever the underlying cause for the peripheral neuropathic pain symptomatologies that Liam has…”

That sentence contains the whole journey.

The uncertainty is still there.

Whatever the underlying cause.

But notice what is no longer uncertain.

The pain is already neuropathic.

The category has become the fixed part of the sentence. Only its cause remains open.

That is not where the story began.

In 2017 the question was whether the residual leg and foot symptoms might be separate, perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy, perhaps related in some respect to alcohol.

Not clear.

The uncertainty originally surrounded the category itself.

Was this neuropathy?

By the time of the complaint response, the uncertainty had moved downstream.

What is causing this neuropathic pain?

That movement is the mechanism in miniature.

A possibility can survive long enough that later uncertainty no longer threatens it. The institution remains uncertain, but only inside the walls of the structure already built.

The response did something similar with Sinemet.

The record had become increasingly clear that Sinemet changed the pain. By March 2025 it could say I knew very well that when Sinemet hit my system the feet felt better. It could say more Sinemet would probably provide further relief. After controlled-release Sinemet produced the dramatic morning response, the later defence could acknowledge profound relief.

But the meaning of that response was contained in advance.

Sinemet could alter the experience of pain without the response being allowed to identify the pain.

The drug was therefore permitted to modify the phenomenon without being permitted to challenge the category in which the phenomenon had been stored.

That is an extraordinarily useful arrangement for an inherited explanation.

If Lyrica helped, that was consistent with neuropathic pain.

If maximum-dose Lyrica did not eliminate the pain, there were other neuropathic drugs to consider.

If reducing Lyrica was followed by worsening pain, that supported restoring Lyrica.

If Sinemet improved the feet, Parkinson’s could be said to improve the experience of pain “of whatever cause.”

If controlled-release Sinemet produced profound relief, that too could be understood as modulation of pain rather than evidence requiring the old partition to be rebuilt.

The explanation could therefore survive evidence in both directions.

That is the point at which uncertainty stops functioning as humility and begins functioning as architecture.

The complaint response did not invent the history. That would be too easy an accusation and, in important ways, false. The letters existed. The medications had been prescribed. The dyskinesia was real. The Lyrica history was real. The pain was real. The benefit from Sinemet was real.

The problem was not fabricated facts.

It was the relationship among the facts.

The record was dense with truth and still capable of telling the wrong story.

That is why “as the letters show” matters so much.

The letters did show something.

They showed continuity.

But continuity is not convergence.

A category repeated from one letter to the next can become more documentary without becoming more evidentially independent. A later letter can cite the treatment created by an earlier hypothesis as evidence that the hypothesis belonged there. A medication prescribed because the pain had been called neuropathic can later become part of the proof that neuropathic pain was being treated. The pathway generates the history which then validates the pathway.

The institution allows the error to become the solution to itself.
By the time I understood that, I had stopped arguing over isolated phrases.

The isolated phrases were often defensible.

“Perhaps.”

“Not clear.”

“If there is a degree of peripheral neuropathy.”

“Of whatever cause.”

Each phrase, taken alone, performs caution.

Placed in sequence, they show something more severe.

The uncertainty never disappears.

It changes jobs.

At the beginning, uncertainty should have kept the whole lower-limb problem open.

Later, uncertainty protected the peripheral branch from being falsified.

Still later, uncertainty allowed the institution to defend that branch without ever having to say that peripheral neuropathy had actually been established.

The language remained cautious enough to avoid final commitment and stable enough to organise years of care.

It survived by organising uncertainty.

This is why I do not need to accuse every later clinician of making the original mistake.

Most of them could not have made it.

They were not there.

They inherited the chart.

A doctor who never met me in 2017 could not inherit my feet. They inherited the words used for my feet.

Once the words had travelled far enough, provenance became invisible.

The later reader did not see a frightened patient telling a neurologist behind a curtain that he was about to be sent out after a full NCT on bald tyres.

They saw peripheral neuropathy.

They did not see eight days in which the feet had not received the investigation I expected when I entered hospital.

They saw a history of foot pain.

They did not see the moment the relationship between the feet and the Parkinsonian syndrome was cut.

They saw two branches of a case.

They did not see the knife.

The person who could see all of that was the person who had written the first separation.

That is where the complaint became something more painful than disagreement.

When the response cited the letters, the letters were not neutral witnesses standing outside the dispute.

They were descendants of the disputed event.

I had asked how the house had been built.

The answer showed me the rooms.

# Chapter Eighteen — The Only One Who Knew

Watch what disappears when the case enters the hospital.

Then watch what becomes painstakingly explainable when the pathway later has to account for itself.

Between those two moments lies the anatomy of what happened to me.

My GP did not send Parkinson’s disease to Neurology.

He sent a trajectory.

The feet were old. The Parkinsonian component was new. The referral carried both. It carried years of foot trouble, clawing and gait difficulty alongside newer deterioration in dexterity, movement, tone and function. The task was not merely to identify Parkinsonism. It was to understand what had changed in a man whose lower limbs had already been wrong for years.

The hospital did something important and did it well.

It recognised Parkinsonism.

It saw a strikingly abnormal gait. It described an almost foot-drop appearance but explicitly recognised that it was not foot drop and might instead represent dystonic posture. It investigated serious neurological disease. It ordered imaging. It thought about young-onset and genetic Parkinsonism. It discussed the case. The diagnostic sophistication rose quickly.

At the same time, the relationship among the symptoms narrowed.

The feet did not vanish as facts.

They vanished as a relationship.

That distinction is everything.

The feet survived as corns, callouses, clawing, hammertoes, pain, podiatry, possible neuropathy, alcohol, burning, neuropathic symptoms, toe curling and later peripheral neuropathic pain symptomatologies.

The Parkinsonism survived as Parkinsonism, abnormal DAT imaging, dystonia, young-onset disease, genetics, PARK2, PARK9, medication response and movement-disorders theory.

Both stories became richer.

They became richer apart.

The case became diagnostically more sophisticated and clinically narrower at the same time.

The patient's Parkinsonism went upstairs.

His feet went out the side door.

The tragedy is not that Galway lacked the ability to think about unusual Parkinsonism.

That would make the story simpler.

The relevant expertise was nearby. The case reached a Complex Case Meeting. Senior neurological expertise entered the discussion. Monogenic Parkinsonism was considered. Parkin disease was considered. PARK9 was considered precisely because the record contained what it called “the neuropathy question.” The institution was capable of thinking at a high level about rare disease, genetics, dystonia and unusual movement.

The problem was where that sophistication was applied.

It travelled upward into theory rather than downward into phenotype.

The unusual Parkinsonism became intellectually interesting.

The old feet became administratively manageable.

That is the dinosaur which walked into the archaeology office.

A young man arrived carrying what would eventually be understood as a long prodromal neurological history, followed years later by florid Parkinsonism. The institution recognised that something rare might be in front of it. It began assembling the genetic skeleton.

But the oldest bones were put in another box.

For years I thought the central failure was that somebody had missed an answer.

I think differently now.

The deeper failure was that the question itself was divided.

Once that happened, later medicine could become very good at answering the questions it had created.

What caused the Parkinsonism?

How should it be treated?

Which monogenic form might fit?

Was there a neuropathy?

Could alcohol contribute?

Could Fabry disease explain Parkinsonism and neuropathic symptoms?

Which drug should treat the pain?

How much levodopa could be given before dyskinesia became unacceptable?

All of those are legitimate questions.

But legitimacy downstream does not repair a bad foundation upstream.

The foundation was the relationship.

Were the feet part of the same neurological trajectory or not?

That question was never answered with equivalent seriousness.

It was operationalised before it was resolved.

That is why the record could become so impressive and still remain structurally unsound.

A house can contain sound doors, good windows, expensive wiring and beautifully engineered stairs and still fail if the foundation is wrong.

The individual notes are not the house.

The relationship among them is.

This is also why the person who wrote the original synthesis occupies a unique position in the story.

Every later clinician inherited information.

He inherited memory.

He knew that “perhaps” had been perhaps.

He knew that “Not clear” had been not clear.

He knew that the patient had raised the feet before discharge.

He knew what had and had not been done before the feet were redescribed as separate.

He knew the provenance of “the neuropathy question” because he had participated in creating it.

Everybody else knew the record.

The neurologist knew the provenance.

That is the information asymmetry which took me years to understand.

I had assumed that because I possessed the lived history, I possessed the important part of the case. In one sense I did. I knew what hurt. I knew what changed with medication. I knew what walking felt like. I knew when the feet became burning feet and when they had not been burning feet. I knew what happened when procyclidine stopped. I knew what happened when Sinemet came on. I knew what happened when controlled release changed the morning.

But I did not know what later clinicians were being told about those things.

I did not know which uncertainty had been preserved and which had been erased.

I did not know that the same fact could be carried forward while its original relationship to the rest of the case disappeared.

Facts can survive compression while relationships do not.

That may be the single most important thing I learned from reading my own medical record.

A record can be factually dense and relationally false.

A patient can recognise almost every event described and still fail to recognise the story the events have been made to tell.

In 2026, genetics finally supplied another piece of the history which had been suspected for years. Testing identified two pathogenic variants in the Parkin gene. The specialist conclusion was that my Parkinsonism and dystonia were due to compound heterozygous mutations in PRKN.

I do not use that result as a magic wand over the past.

It does not prove that every corn, every callus, every painful step or every later burning sensation came from one mechanism. It does not abolish the reality of structural foot disease. It does not retrospectively convert every uncertainty into certainty.

It does something more disciplined.

It returns dystonia to the centre of the disease which the hospital had been trying to understand from the beginning.

The old genetic suspicion was not foolish. The movement-disorders reasoning was not foolish. The tragedy is that the most unusual part of the disease became more sophisticated while the oldest bodily history remained partitioned from it.

The genetics did not rescue the argument.

They exposed the cost of the original separation.

By then I had also understood something about the route I did not want to take.

I could try to convert this history into a conventional legal dispute. I could employ solicitors, barristers and experts. They could argue about breach of duty, causation, standards of care, damages and probabilities. The hospital could defend the notes. Experts could disagree about what the notes mean. Perhaps the case would fight its way toward court. Perhaps somebody would want to settle it. I do not know.

I am not going to invent that future in order to make this ending dramatic.

The decision I can make is about me.

I do not want the meaning of these years reduced to a negotiation over what they are worth.

I do not want to spend the next part of my life arguing sentence by sentence with people whose explanation I have already spent eight years living inside.

And I will not exchange the story for silence.

If somebody offered compensation on the condition that I sign away the right to tell this history, I know which one I would keep.

The history.

That is why this book exists.

It is not a pleading with chapters attached.

It is not a complaint response with better adjectives.

It is an attempt to restore relationships among facts which the record kept but separated.

The patient arrived with sore feet.

The feet went missing in the compression of the case.

The patient put them back in front of Neurology before discharge and exposed a failure between the problem referred and the problem being answered.

Something then had to happen.

There was a systems failure to repair, but there was also still a patient standing there with sore feet.

The system answered both problems with one move.

The residual feet were placed elsewhere.

Perhaps peripheral neuropathy.

Perhaps alcohol.

Not clear.

That formulation allowed the institutional problem to close without requiring the patient's problem to be solved.

Then the formulation began to live.

The feet changed under neurological treatment.

That fact travelled badly.

The neuropathy question travelled well.

The toes returned.

The body changed.

Procyclidine changed the feet.

Levodopa changed the feet.

OFF states changed the feet.

Dyskinesia changed the way I moved on the feet.

Pregabalin reached maximum dose without eliminating the pain.

The pain became psychologically dangerous.

The patient asked for help.

And on the last prescription sheet from the neurologist who had known the case from the beginning, a safeguard was written for constipation while the same sheet carried the controlled-release Sinemet that was about to transform the mornings.

The last safeguard was for constipation.

The last prescription contained the contradiction.

The following morning the body answered.

After that, the record could still explain itself. It could cite letters. It could describe pain modulation. It could preserve neuropathic symptomatology while leaving its cause open. It could point to Lyrica, dyskinesia, caution and clinical judgment.

But the body had become an amendment the record could no longer fully absorb.

The patient had found the foundation.

I have no interest now in pretending that certainty is the answer to uncertainty.

Medicine needs uncertainty. Law needs uncertainty. Any serious account of another human being needs uncertainty. “I don't know” can be one of the most honest sentences in the language.

The danger begins when uncertainty is distributed unevenly.

When the chosen explanation is allowed to remain stable while only the alternatives must keep proving themselves.

When “perhaps” is strong enough to organise treatment but too weak to require confirmation.

When later contradiction is absorbed locally instead of reopening the foundation.

When the record becomes more certain about what category the patient belongs in than it is curious about what is happening to him now.

That is the failure mode this hospital had an opportunity to discover.

Not merely an error in one man's diagnosis.

A way in which an institution can preserve uncertainty so successfully that the uncertainty becomes durable enough to govern care.

I do not think the answer is to destroy the record.

The record is what allowed me to find this.

I do not think the answer is to distrust doctors.

Trust is what makes medicine possible.

The answer is more demanding.

A record must remain capable of being corrected by the body it describes.

A hypothesis must remain vulnerable to the patient.

A later clinician must be able to ask not only what the chart says, but how the chart came to say it.

The purpose of medicine is not to preserve yesterday's explanation.

It is to understand today's patient.

For eight years, the hospital had the authoritative story and I had the body.

Then I acquired the record too.

I put the two beside each other.

I did not find a secret file. I did not find a confession. I did not find a sentence saying that somebody had knowingly harmed me.

I found something harder to dismiss.

I found the ordinary notes.

I found the ordinary caution.

I found the ordinary inheritance of language.

I found uncertainty becoming pathway, pathway becoming history, and history returning as proof of itself.

I found a patient repeatedly supplying information capable of reopening the case and a record repeatedly finding a place for the information which did not require the case to be reopened.

I found that nobody needed to lie for the wrong story to become durable.

They only needed to keep reading from it.

I have spent enough time asking the people inside that story to tell me what it means.

I do not want to argue with them anymore.

I want to tell everybody else.

The feet were mine before they were a symptom.

The pain was mine before it was a category.

The years were mine while the explanation held.

And this story is mine now.
